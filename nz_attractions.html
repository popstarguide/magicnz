Abel Tasman National Park

Things to do

Abel Tasman National Park

The National Park is easily accessible from either Marahau or Takaka ends. There is multi-day or one-day hike available in the park. Often the one-day, one-way hike from Torrent or Bark Bay is a great way to spend the day regardless of time of year.

The one-day hike starts with a water taxi ride to your start point then follows the coast back to where you’ve left or pick up your transport home. Relatively easy walk with no serious climbs or difficult terrain to traverse.

Sea kayaks

Sea kayaking is one of the very best ways to experience Abel Tasman National Park — golden beaches, sculpted granite headlands, and calm, sheltered water, all best seen from the seat of a kayak. Several operators run guided trips from Marahau or Kaiteriteri, ranging from a couple of hours right through to multi-day guided expeditions, taking in highlights like Split Apple Rock, sea caves, and the seal colonies around Tonga and Adele Islands. No experience is needed — guides give you full training before you set off, and the coastline here is about as forgiving as sea kayaking gets. If you'd rather go it alone, kayak hire is also widely available for those wanting to explore at their own pace.

Abel Tasman water Taxi

Water taxis are one of the most flexible and popular ways to get around Abel Tasman National Park, running from Marahau and, in the busier months, Kaiteriteri, out to the six main bays along the coast — Anchorage, Torrent Bay, Bark Bay, Onetahuti, Awaroa, and Tōtaranui. They're purpose-built for the park's shallow, tidal waters, so as well as being quick and scenic, they can get right in close to beaches and hidden spots larger boats simply can't reach. Water taxis give you total flexibility to design your own day — start a walk in one bay and finish in another, get dropped somewhere remote for a picnic, or combine a short walk with a scenic ride back. Just be ready for wet feet getting on and off, since most drop-offs happen right on the beach.

Abel Tasman Bus

If you're not self-driving, getting to Abel Tasman National Park is easy thanks to regular bus services connecting Nelson and Motueka to Kaiteriteri and Marahau — the two main gateways into the park. A few operators run this route, generally timed to connect with water taxi and boat departures, so you can book bus, boat and any walks or tours all in one go. It's a relaxed way to start the day, taking in some lovely scenery along the way, and means you don't need to worry about parking or driving back tired at the end of a big day out on the water or the track.

One Tree Hill

One (None) Tree Hill work the trip more because of its history as well as the views.

Originally named Maungakiekie (trans; Mountain of the kiekie, or Totara that stands alone)

The site was once home to the largest Māori Pa (fortified village) ever built pre-European times, home to 5000 inhabitants. Just prior to the European arrival the Māori had largely left the area due to tribal battles in the north. Although time has played its part today it is still easy to see the earthworks that were created as a by-product of the Pa.

Once the early European settlers settled the area one of Auckland's founding fathers John Logan Campbell owned the hill. Originally planning to farm the hill he changed his mind when he saw the rapid development of the area for housing and infrastructure, so created the reserve Cornwall Park.

During this period there was a Pohutukawa tree growing near the summit of the hill, due to vandalism or the need for firewood the tree was cut down. The original tree which gave the hill its name was replaced by a Californian Radiata Pine in the 1870’s.

The tree has been under attack in differing forms by Māori activists trying to make their political point known over the years. The only successful attack came in October 2000 by activist Mike Smith who used a chainsaw to try to remove the pine, however a chainsaw at 2am is easily heard and he was prevented from finishing his cut. The damage he had done was enough that the tree died and had to be removed by the local council.

The hill is also honoured in song, most famously by the Irish group U2 in their song One Tree Hill, written in honour of their Kiwi roadie Greg Carroll who was killed in a motorcycle accident while they were writing the Joshua Tree album. The song was only released in New Zealand and promptly went to number 1 on the music charts.

MOTAT (Museum of Transport and Technology)

The museum located a short bus trip from down town pays homage to all things mechanical that have a connection with New Zealand.

Spread over 2 locations joined by a restored Auckland tram line the grounds bracket the Auckland Zoo. As well as all the usual things you would expect to find (trucks, tractors, busses, steam engines, planes...)

MOTAT houses some very unique pieces; a replica of the first powered aircraft, built from the plans of the inventor Richard Pearce and arguably the first person to achieve a controlled powered flight,

Displays about aviation pioneers Jean Batten and the Walsh brothers, a Lancaster bomber and a replica of Sir Keith Parks (the defender of London in WW2) Hurricane fighter.

Kelly Tarltons

Kelly Tarltons Aquarium. Situated a short bus trip east of the downtown is the by-product of New Zealand's own diver/ adventurer Kelly Tarlton.

Kelly Tarltons dream was to bring the Southern Oceans life as viewed by divers to the everyday public with out getting their feet wet.

Converting a disused sewer treatment plant to a giant aquarium Kelly made his dream come true in 1985. Kelly died only a couple of months after its opening but he did get a glimpse of how successful it was to really become, as the same day he died the 10,000 visitors passed through its doors.

Here is a fantastic opportunity to see the ocean alive with out having to pass a dive certificate or don a wetsuit.

Hundertwasser Toilets

Friedensreich Hundertwasser, an Austrian-born artist, lived in Kawakawa until his death in 2000. He designed these unique public toilets, which have become a tourist destination in their own right. Hundertwasser designed a number of unique buildings that mostly reside in Europe.

He also gifted New Zealand an alternative flag design. It was not adopted, nor was it allowed to be entered in the flag referendum in 2016. Hundertwasser's flag incorporated representations of the indigenous Māori, the Pākehā influence, the land and new growth.

Top 5

Cardboard Cathedral

On 22 February 2011, a magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck Christchurch. It's considered one of New Zealand's deadliest disasters, killing 185 people and doing enormous damage to the city's infrastructure. The city's landmark Anglican ChristChurch Cathedral was among the buildings badly damaged, and with its future uncertain, the Anglican Diocese needed somewhere to worship in the meantime. What they built is what's known as the Cardboard Cathedral — a fast, striking, and comparatively cheap solution, designed by Japanese architect Shigeru Ban using cardboard tubes, timber and steel. It's since become an iconic landmark in its own right, and well worth a visit.

Antarctic Centre

US and New Zealand Antarctic operations are run out of Christchurch, supporting bases in the Ross Sea and McMurdo Sound, Antarctica. Being the launching point for both nations' programmes, it only makes sense that the city is home to the International Antarctic Centre, celebrating the efforts on the ice. It's a fantastic, hands-on experience — highlights include the Hägglund ride, a thrilling spin in a genuine Antarctic all-terrain vehicle, and the Storm Room, where you can feel Antarctic conditions for yourself, temperatures down around -8°C with wind chill dropping further still.

Dunedin

Things to do

Elm Wildlife Tours

Top 5

Elm Wildlife tour at Taiaroa Heads near Dunedin. For around the $180 the tour shows you the Royal Southern Albatross (the largest flying bird in the world), the yellow-eyed penguin (one of the rarest penguins in the world), the Hooker sea lion (native to the southern coast) and the Kekeno the New Zealand fur seal (found all round the south island but still fun to watch). The tour shows you rare and endangered wildlife unique to the this region in their own environment, no zoo’s or reserves. The tour lasts 4 hours plus and is held every afternoon. The guides are knowledgeable and patient.

Baldwin St

One of Dunedin's most famous landmarks is Baldwin Street, officially recognised by Guinness World Records as the world's steepest street. It's had a bit of a rollercoaster with the title — it held the record for decades, briefly lost it to a street in Wales in 2019, then reclaimed it in 2020 after a review of how steepness should be measured. At its steepest, the street climbs at a gradient of nearly 35%, rising about a metre for every 2.86 metres you walk.

Larnich Castle

Local business entrepreneur William Larnach looked around at the surroundings on the Otago Peninsula and thought, "I know what this town needs — a castle." So in 1871, he set about building one, styled on the grand castles of the UK, using materials shipped in from around the world and around 200 labourers. It's now New Zealand's only castle. Guided and self-guided tours take you through this grand building, along with its extensive gardens.

Olveston House

David Theomin was a wealthy Dunedin merchant and collector in the late 1800s and early 1900s. He and his wife Marie travelled widely, and immersed themselves in gathering a unique collection of art, furniture and curios from around the world. Their house — Olveston, built between 1904 and 1907 — has since been turned into a museum, dressed up to show what each of its 35 rooms would have looked like in the height of its Edwardian splendour. You'll see amazing trinkets, works by globally recognised artists like Charles Goldie and Frances Hodgkins, and stunning silverware, crockery and furniture. It's a unique time capsule, and well worth visiting.

Albatross Colony

One of the unique natural wonders of New Zealand is the Northern Royal Albatross. This albatross circumnavigates the globe, but the only place in the world it touches land to breed is Taiaroa Head, on the Otago Peninsula. The Royal Albatross Centre will take you through more information on the albatross, and if you take the viewing option, you'll head up to a hide where you can look out across the nesting area. If you're there at the right time of year, you'll see the chicks starting to attempt their first flight. A pretty unique and awesome place to visit.

Things to do

Wainui Beach can have days where the surf is only for experienced surfers, and other days where beginners to intermediates can have fun out there too. Cook's Memorial

History is often written by whoever holds the pen. Just as Columbus is credited with “discovering” America, despite the First Nations people who were already there, Captain Cook is remembered as the Englishman who claimed New Zealand for the Crown — with little regard, at the time, for the Māori who called it home. Encouragingly, the monument at Gisborne’s Cook Landing site has evolved with our understanding of that history. Alongside the original obelisk, it now features the Ikaroa sculpture, honouring the early Māori navigators Māia and Puhi-Kai-Iti, and nine pou remembering the Māori killed during Cook’s first, fraught encounter with local iwi. It’s a site that now tells both sides of the story.

Early Explorer Statues

Down by the port, where the Tūranganui River meets Waikanae Beach, three statues honour early figures connected to the region. The first, the Hawaiki Tūranga sculpture, represents Ruapani, a paramount chief with connections to all the local iwi of Tairāwhiti. Next along is Captain Cook, and a little further on, pointing out toward the headland that bears his name, is Nicholas Young — “Young Nick” — the Endeavour’s surgeon’s boy who first sighted the New Zealand coast.

Footrot Flats Statues

For any New Zealander born before the 2000s, “Footrot Flats” is just part of the vernacular. The strip follows a farm that only ever existed in the imagination of its creator, cartoonist Murray Ball. Ball found early recognition while based in the UK, with strips like “Stanley the Palaeolithic Hero” — the longest-running strip Punch magazine ever published. Returning to Gisborne, he bought a small farm, and from there created Footrot Flats. It ran in newspapers across New Zealand and Australia, was collected into more than 40 published books, and by the late ’80s had become a full-length animated feature film. Wal Footrot and his sheepdog, Dog, are the central characters, with much of the strip’s humour seen through Dog’s eyes. To get your photo with Wal and Dog, look for the bronze statue right beside the library in the middle of town. https://footrotflats.com/

Hawke's Bay Farmers' Market

If you’re in Hawke’s Bay and around Hastings on a Sunday, getting along to the farmers’ market is an absolute must. It’s one of the oldest and largest in the country, running since 2000, held in the tree-lined Waikoko Gardens at Tomoana Showgrounds. Pick up some fresh local produce, or maybe a craft piece that catches your eye to take home as a memento. Entry is free, running from 8:30am to 12:30pm every Sunday.

With over a hundred wineries making up the Hawke’s Bay wine region, not all of them have cellar doors, and not all cellar doors are open to the public. If you want to sample wines from the region, pop into the i-SITE, where you can pick up a free map showing which cellar doors are open, along with their hours and days.

Cape Kidnappers Gannet Colony

East of Hastings lies Cape Kidnappers, home to the world’s largest, most accessible mainland gannet colony — up to 20,000 birds nest here between September and April, before migrating to warmer climates. You can walk out to the colony yourself along the beach from Clifton, but the track is genuinely treacherous — it’s tide-dependent, and prone to slips that can take away sections of the path or bury it entirely. Your best option is one of the guided tours, whether by 4x4 or tractor. Not only do you get an experienced guide and their local knowledge, but they’ll take their own well-worn route to and from the colony, avoiding the worst of the hazards.

Bridge Pa Wine Triangle

Bridge Pa is a region within the Hawke’s Bay wine region, known as the Bridge Pa Triangle for the three roads that frame it. Along these three roads you’ll find eleven member wineries. You can reach the area by catching a public bus out and doing a cycle tour, or drive yourself — though driving raises the obvious problem: how does the driver get to sample the wine and still stay sober enough to get everyone home safely? A cycle tour, or arranging a designated driver, solves that one nicely.

Mangatainoka

Things to do

Tui Brewery

From 1889 until 2021, Tui Brewery was brewing beer at its Mangatainoka site. The iconic seven-storey building, built in 1931, was designed as a gravity-fed system — raw materials went in at the top, and bottled beer came out the bottom. When it was near completion, they discovered a major flaw: there was no lift or stairs to get to the top. The café, bar, and visitor centre now on site, known as Tui HQ, is a byproduct of Tui’s famously irreverent “Yeah Right” advertising campaign, launched in 1997. A long-running series of billboards would make a bold statement, then undercut it with the tagline “Yeah right.” You can pick up a book of some of the best billboards at the café.

Napier

Things to do

Art Deco Guided Walking Tour

Art Deco is a style of architecture designed to be strong, sturdy, and plain — and understanding what makes it unique is often far easier with a guided tour. Napier’s Art Deco Trust runs 1.5 to 2-hour guided walks through the city’s Art Deco Quarter, starting with a short film on the devastating 1931 earthquake that levelled Napier and led to its rebuild in this very style. You’ll see banks, fire stations, showplaces, and shopfronts along the way. What makes Napier’s Art Deco truly one-of-a-kind is the Kiwi twist: local architects wove Māori motifs into the design, blending zigzags and patterns from Māori art with the classic Art Deco style. Your guide will point these details out as you go — a fantastic way to explore an art style found nowhere else in quite the same form.

Art Deco Self-Guided Walking Tour

If you’d rather explore at your own pace, the Art Deco Trust also offers a self-guided walking map of Napier’s Art Deco Quarter. It’s a handy way to see the key buildings without booking a set tour time, though you’ll miss out on the stories and insider details a live guide brings — you’re really just following the map and reading as you go.

Geographical Centre of NZ

On top of Botanical Hill, on the edge of Nelson city, sits a monument marking the "Centre of New Zealand" — though despite the name, it isn't quite the true geographical centre (that honour actually belongs to a spot in the Golden Downs Forest, south of Kohatu). What it does mark is the historic trig point from which the very first triangulated surveys of the country began back in the 1870s. It's a technically easy 20-to-30-minute walk up the hill from the Botanical Reserve, through poplar trees and stands of kauri, and well worth the climb for sweeping views over Nelson, Tasman Bay, and the surrounding hills.

Tahunanui Beach

Tāhunanui Beach is Nelson's main beach, and for good reason — a gently sloping, 1.75km stretch of sand just a few minutes from the city centre. The name comes from the Māori for "big sandbank," which sums it up nicely. It's a genuine family playground: safe swimming, sunbathing, paddleboarding, and kitesurfing on the water, with a large family-friendly reserve behind the dunes packed with playgrounds, mini golf, and other activities, plus the adjoining Tāhuna Beach Holiday Park if you're after somewhere to stay right on the sand. At low tide, the eastern end reveals rockpools full of crabs and small fish, and there's good fishing off the pier if you're that way inclined. An easy, relaxed spot to spend an afternoon.

New Plymouth

Things to do

Mt Taranaki

Mt Taranaki is the iconic landmark that not only overlooks the province but is also Egmont National Park. The national park has two visitor centres and many hikes, ranging from 30-minute 'easy' walks to multi-day epic hikes.

Thanks to a photo taken by local photographer Rob Tucker, the view of the mountain from The Tarn has become one of the shots people want to recreate for themselves. If you're of moderate fitness and well prepared, this is a doable achievement — though lining up good weather may be more problematic.

The mountain can be summited as a day hike, though calling it a 'day hike' massively understates how challenging it can be. Summiting from the North Egmont Visitor Centre (the most common route) involves around four hours of climbing and three of descent. On a stunning day, most people of average fitness will feel it.

The Dawson Falls walk is a relatively short walk to see the waterfall — not too challenging, though there are steps to climb, and it has great views over South Taranaki.

Mt Taranaki is considered one of the most deadly mountains in New Zealand because of its accessibility — a lot of people underestimate how quickly conditions can change and find themselves woefully underprepared. Please read the bush/hike safety section in this app and talk with local DOC staff before exploring; they'll be happy to suggest good options for your ability level or advise on what you could be walking into that day.

Daisy Day Learn to Surf

Daisy Day, champion longboard rider, offers learn-to-surf lessons at the local break, Fitzroy. Daisy has been coaching and teaching surfing since the early 2000s. For years her school set up camp daily during summer; since COVID lockdowns, lessons have become more on-demand. Pop into the local surf shop, Reach the Beach, and they should be able to put you in contact with Daisy's school.

Coastal Walkway

Started from an idea by a small group in the 1990s to provide a multi-use avenue of access aimed at moving skateboarders and cyclists off the streets and footpaths, the Coastal Walkway they convinced the council to build has become an iconic drawcard for the city. It now links the western suburb of Ngāmotu with the eastern suburb of Bell Block, spanning the city's coastal foreshore. In all weathers, the sea provides awesome vistas.

Pukekura Park

Pukekura Park is a 52ha (128 acre) park in the heart of downtown New Plymouth, with lush native and introduced flora, a fernery, two main lakes, row boats, a cafe, an international cricket ground, an outdoor concert venue and the local zoo — the park has plenty to see and do.

During the day the park is a pleasant escape from the city, but during the summer school holidays, at night the park comes alive. Light displays have been part of summer in the park since the 1970s, and in 1993 it officially became the Festival of Lights. It's won awards since, as it tries to outdo itself year on year.

The cricket ground, while holding international status, is one of the smallest grounds internationally, so it doesn't often host international matches. Local representative teams play many home games at the venue through summer, and it's drawn comments from visitors describing it as one of the most attractive grounds imaginable.

The Bowl of Brooklands — or simply 'the Bowl' as it's known locally — is home to the local zoo and a natural amphitheatre. At the base of the amphitheatre is the stage, set against the iconic lake the venue is known for. The venue biennially hosts WOMAD, a massive international festival of music, arts and dance that runs for three days. If you're in town over those three days, book everything from accommodation to taxis well ahead.

Govett-Brewster / Len Lye Art Gallery

Len Lye was among a handful of internationally acclaimed artists working in kinetic (moving) art. Though not from New Plymouth, when he passed away his remaining works were bequeathed to his friend Monica Brewster for her gallery, and the Govett-Brewster has become the keeper of Lye's legacy. In mid-2015 the gallery finished its multi-million-dollar expansion with the opening of the Len Lye Centre. The centre always has a few of Lye's works on display, but even if you're not into art, the building itself — clad entirely in stainless steel, one of Lye's materials of choice — is worth admiring.

NZ Natural Clothing

Norsewear was started by Ola Rian, a Norwegian army captain who fell in love with New Zealand on a skiing holiday, married a Kiwi woman, Shirley, and began making Scandinavian-style ski hats and socks from New Zealand wool as a side hustle back in 1963. That’s where the now-iconic “Farm Fleck” sock was born, and it’s been in continuous production ever since. While brands like Norsewear, Swanndri, and others have faced real competition in recent years from lighter synthetic materials, they’ve held onto their reputation thanks to the warmth, quality, and craftsmanship of their garments. The Norsewear factory itself sits right behind NZ Natural Clothing, though the two have no financial connection. Still, you’ll find Norsewear, Swanndri, and other iconic New Zealand woollen brands on the shelves inside — a quick stop into the shop often turns up bargains on quality wool garments. Omaka WW1 Aviation museum

Just outside Blenheim, the Omaka Aviation Heritage Centre houses one of the world's largest collections of World War I aircraft, most of it on long-term loan from film director Sir Peter Jackson, himself a keen collector of Great War memorabilia. What makes Omaka so different from a typical aviation museum is the way it's presented — the "Knights of the Sky" exhibition uses full-scale, movie-quality dioramas created by WingNut Films, with lifelike mannequins from Weta Workshop, bringing WWI dogfights and battlefield scenes to life around the genuine aircraft. A second exhibition, "Dangerous Skies," opened in 2016 and covers WWII, including a rare de Havilland Mosquito and a Spitfire. It's genuinely one of the best-presented museums in the country, worth a couple of hours whether you're an aviation buff or not.

Marlborough wine

Marlborough is New Zealand's largest and most important wine region, producing more grapes than the rest of the country combined — and it's almost entirely built on one grape: Sauvignon Blanc. Commercial viticulture here only really began in 1973, when Montana Wines (now Brancott Estate) planted the region's first large-scale vineyards. It took off from there — by 1985, Cloudy Bay's Sauvignon Blanc had won international acclaim, redefining the variety and putting Marlborough on the world wine map. Today over three-quarters of the region's vineyard land is planted in Sauvignon Blanc, thriving here thanks to a cool, sunny, maritime climate with big swings between warm days and cool nights, which helps preserve the grape's punchy, zesty character. The region splits into three main sub-areas — the historic Wairau Valley, the Southern Valleys, and the Awatere Valley — each producing a slightly different style, and with well over 100 wineries and cellar doors to explore, there's no shortage of ways to taste your way through it.

Sounds connection wine tours

Sounds Connection runs guided wine tours through the Marlborough region, with pick-ups from both Picton and Blenheim. Half-day and full-day options take you to a handful of hand-selected wineries — typically three to seven, depending on the tour — sampling the region's Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, and other varieties along the way, with a knowledgeable local guide sharing the history and stories behind the wines as you go. It's a relaxed, no-pressure way to explore Marlborough's wineries without needing to worry about the driving, and complimentary pick-up is available from most central Picton and Blenheim accommodation, as well as the ferry terminals and airports.

Denniston

Things to do

Denniston mine

High on the Denniston Plateau above Westport once sat New Zealand's largest producing coal mine, accessed by an extraordinary feat of engineering: the Denniston Incline. Opened in 1879 and locally dubbed "the eighth wonder of the world," the incline dropped a staggering 518 metres over just 1.7 kilometres of track, hauling coal wagons down by gravity, with descending loaded wagons pulling the empty ones back up. It carried an estimated 12 million tonnes of coal down the hill before finally closing in 1967. The views from up on the incline genuinely have to be seen to be believed — photos simply don't do it justice. It's easy to see why one resident, after riding the incline up to Denniston, famously said there was only one way she was getting in to go back down again. Life on the exposed, windswept plateau was famously grim for the miners and their families, giving the town a reputation as a place either loved or hated. Today, the Denniston Experience runs underground mine tours, and walking trails through the historic area let you explore the remains of the incline and the old mining settlement for yourself.

Harihari

Things to do

Guy Lambton Menzies

On 7 January 1931, 21-year-old Australian aviator Guy Lambton Menzies took off from Sydney in a single-engine Avro Avian biplane named "Southern Cross Junior," telling friends he was flying to Perth. In truth, he was attempting a solo crossing of the Tasman Sea, unauthorised and alone, carrying nothing more than a spare collar, a razor, and a toothbrush. After nearly 12 hours battling rough weather, he spotted the West Coast and, mistaking a swamp near Harihari for a grassy paddock, brought his plane down — flipping it upside down on landing. He climbed out with barely a scratch, having just become the first person to fly solo across the Tasman. Locals were initially skeptical of his story, until he produced a sandwich bag from Sydney airport as proof. The landing site is now marked with a roadside memorial and a full-size replica of his plane, in Guy Menzies Park at the western end of Harihari township — an easy stop right off the highway.

Marlborough

Marlborough wine

Marlborough is New Zealand's largest and most important wine region, producing more grapes than the rest of the country combined — and it's almost entirely built on one grape: Sauvignon Blanc. Commercial viticulture here only really began in 1973, when Montana Wines (now Brancott Estate) planted the region's first large-scale vineyards. It took off from there — by 1985, Cloudy Bay's Sauvignon Blanc had won international acclaim, redefining the variety and putting Marlborough on the world wine map. Today over three-quarters of the region's vineyard land is planted in Sauvignon Blanc, thriving here thanks to a cool, sunny, maritime climate with big swings between warm days and cool nights, which helps preserve the grape's punchy, zesty character. The region splits into three main sub-areas — the historic Wairau Valley, the Southern Valleys, and the Awatere Valley — each producing a slightly different style, and with well over 100 wineries and cellar doors to explore, there's no shortage of ways to taste your way through it.

Okarito

Things to do

Okarito

Just north of Franz Josef is Ōkārito, a tiny, peaceful coastal settlement sitting on New Zealand's largest unmodified wetland lagoon. It's a genuine wildlife haven — over 76 bird species have been recorded here, including the kōtuku (white heron), royal spoonbill, and the incredibly rare Ōkārito brown kiwi, or rowi, found nowhere else in the world. The best way to experience the lagoon is by kayak, gliding quietly across calm water with the Southern Alps reflected on a clear day, birds feeding in the shallows all around you. New Zealand's only kōtuku breeding colony is nearby at the Waitangiroto Nature Reserve, active from around September to January each year; the reserve itself is closed to the public, but White Heron Sanctuary Tours run guided trips during the season, taking you in to view the birds from a hide. Whether you join a guided lagoon kayak tour or time your visit for the herons, it's one of the more understated, tranquil experiences on the West Coast.

Punakaiki

Things to do

Punakaiki - Pancake Rocks

At Dolomite Point, right on the coast at Punakaiki, are the aptly named Pancake Rocks — dramatic, layered limestone formations that genuinely resemble stacks of pancakes, formed some 30 million years ago from marine sediment compressed on the seabed and gradually lifted and carved by wind, rain, and sea. Add the blowholes into the mix, and you've got one of the most photographed spots on the West Coast: when a big swell hits at high tide, the sea forces its way through underground caverns and erupts skyward in dramatic bursts of spray. An easy 20-minute loop walk on a formed path takes you through the formations and out to the blowholes, and it's well worth timing your visit around high tide for the best show.

Ross

Things to do

Gold Panning

Gold was first discovered on the West Coast in 1864, sparking a rush that turned Hokitika into a boom town with over 70 pubs. Modern-day fortune seekers can still try their luck — a good few designated recreational fossicking spots dot the coast, and several places offer a hands-on introduction if you don't fancy going it alone. Shantytown Heritage Park, near Greymouth, has a working sluice line where kids and adults alike are near-guaranteed to find a few golden flecks. In Hokitika, Polish & Pan offers a similar guaranteed-gold experience, right in town. Further up the coast at Ross, the Ross Goldfields Information & Heritage Centre lets you hire a pan and head out to Jones Creek, where gold was first struck in 1865, or pan undercover at the centre if the weather turns — panning undercover at Ross is almost certain to strike gold. However you do it, coming away with a genuine speck of West Coast gold is a pretty fun souvenir.

Pounamu

Pounamu — greenstone, or New Zealand jade — holds deep spiritual significance for Māori, far beyond its value as a souvenir. Found only on the South Island's West Coast, it's the reason the island was originally named Te Wāhi Pounamu, "the place of greenstone." Poutini Ngāi Tahu are the recognised guardians of all pounamu found on the West Coast, and Hokitika, close to the Arahura, has become known as the pounamu capital of New Zealand, home to a thriving community of carvers and artists. You can watch stone workers at their craft in local galleries, join a guided tour along the Arahura River to learn the stories and search the riverbed for a piece of your own, or even try your hand at carving a piece to take home. Traditionally, pounamu is gifted rather than bought for yourself — so if you're after a piece, it makes for a meaningful gift as much as a keepsake. Worth a mention too is Ross, further down the coast, home to artisan carver Steve Maitland, whose shop sits across the road from the i-SITE. His pieces range from around $60 through to the thousands, all hand-crafted and hand-polished — genuinely stunning work. It's worth knowing that cheaper pieces elsewhere often get their shine from a polyurethane coating rather than true hand polishing, so it pays to ask.

Richard Pearse Memorial

This nondescript statue, seemingly in the middle of nowhere, may seem a bit ho-hum. But what's interesting here isn't the memorial itself — it's what happened on this spot. Richard Pearse, considered by many to have flown a powered, heavier-than-air machine before the Wright brothers, did it right here, at a farm miles from anywhere, on 31 March 1903 — nine months ahead of the Wrights' famous flight at Kitty Hawk. Wanaka

Things to do

Lakes District Ski Fields

The Lakes District is home to four major ski fields, all within easy reach of Queenstown and Wanaka. Coronet Peak, the closest to Queenstown, is well-groomed and popular for its lively atmosphere and night skiing. Across the valley, The Remarkables offers sunny, north-facing slopes with terrain for all abilities, plus a good beginners' area. Over towards Wanaka, Cardrona is famous for its reliable snow coverage and wide, gentle slopes, making it a favourite with families and first-timers. It's the furthest of the four to get to from both Queenstown and Wanaka, but it's also the largest, so most days you can still find space to carve out and enjoy the snow. Nearby Treble Cone is the pick for more experienced skiers and snowboarders, with the South Island's biggest vertical drop, steeper terrain, and stunning views over Lake Wanaka. Between the four, there's something to suit every level, and the ski season generally runs from mid-June through to early October.

Warbirds Over Wanaka

Wanaka Airport is home to Warbirds Over Wanaka, the largest warbirds airshow in the Southern Hemisphere. Founded in 1988 by Sir Tim Wallis as a way to showcase his own collection of World War II aircraft, the show has grown into a major biennial event, held over the Easter weekend in even-numbered years, and now draws tens of thousands of visitors from around the world. Expect a genuine mix of history in the sky — Spitfires, Mustangs, and other classic warbirds flying alongside modern jets from air forces including the RNZAF, RAAF and USAF — plus extensive ground displays of vintage aircraft, cars, and military hardware. If your visit lines up with the show, it's an unforgettable few days.

Crossfire Wanaka

Crossfire is a low-cost attraction just on the outskirts of Wanaka, giving you the chance to shoot at things. Choose from clay pigeons, small-bore rifle targets, or take a swing at a golf driving range. There's also a mini golf course on site if you fancy a break from the shooting. If there's a group of you, you can also have a laugh firing tennis balls at each other through an obstacle course.

Puzzling World

Puzzling World, on the outskirts of Wanaka, has been fascinating visitors since 1973, when Stuart and Jan Landsborough sold their house to build a wooden maze on seven acres of barren land. It's since grown into an award-winning complex, home to the world's first "3D Mega Maze," six Illusion Rooms full of optical trickery, and the famous Leaning Tower of Wanaka, tilted at a dizzying 53 degrees. Don't skip the Roman-themed toilets either — they're as much a part of the experience as anything else. A genuinely fun, slightly bizarre stop that's well worth an hour or two.

Siberia Experience

The Siberia Experience is one of the great classic New Zealand adventures — fly, walk, and jet boat, all in one day. It starts with a 25-minute scenic flight out of Makarora aboard "Buttercup," a little yellow bush plane, weaving through the Southern Alps and Mt Aspiring National Park before touching down on a remote grass airstrip in the Siberia Valley. From there, it's a roughly three-hour walk through native beech forest and stunning glacial valley scenery down to the Wilkin River, where a jet boat picks you up for the ride back out to Makarora. A genuinely wild, remote day out, and one of the best ways to experience this part of the national park.

Westport

Things to do

Cape Foulwind/Tauranga Bay

The main draw at Cape Foulwind is the Tauranga Bay Seal Colony Walk — an easy, well-formed 10-minute path from the car park leading to viewing platforms over a resident New Zealand fur seal colony. Seals are present year-round, with the busiest season from November to February when pups are born. For those keen on more of a walk, the full Cape Foulwind Walkway continues along the coastal bluffs all the way to the historic lighthouse, taking around an hour and a half one way, with sweeping views of the rugged coastline the whole way.

Denniston mine

High on the Denniston Plateau above Westport once sat New Zealand's largest producing coal mine, accessed by an extraordinary feat of engineering: the Denniston Incline. Opened in 1879 and locally dubbed "the eighth wonder of the world," the incline dropped a staggering 518 metres over just 1.7 kilometres of track, hauling coal wagons down by gravity, with descending loaded wagons pulling the empty ones back up. It carried an estimated 12 million tonnes of coal down the hill before finally closing in 1967. The views from up on the incline genuinely have to be seen to be believed — photos simply don't do it justice. It's easy to see why one resident, after riding the incline up to Denniston, famously said there was only one way she was getting in to go back down again. Life on the exposed, windswept plateau was famously grim for the miners and their families, giving the town a reputation as a place either loved or hated. Today, the Denniston Experience runs underground mine tours, and walking trails through the historic area let you explore the remains of the incline and the old mining settlement for yourself.

One Tree Hill

One (None) Tree Hill work the trip more because of its history as well as the views.

Originally named Maungakiekie (trans; Mountain of the kiekie, or Totara that stands alone)

The site was once home to the largest Māori Pa (fortified village) ever built pre-European times, home to 5000 inhabitants. Just prior to the European arrival the Māori had largely left the area due to tribal battles in the north. Although time has played its part today it is still easy to see the earthworks that were created as a by-product of the Pa.

Once the early European settlers settled the area one of Auckland's founding fathers John Logan Campbell owned the hill. Originally planning to farm the hill he changed his mind when he saw the rapid development of the area for housing and infrastructure, so created the reserve Cornwall Park.

During this period there was a Pohutukawa tree growing near the summit of the hill, due to vandalism or the need for firewood the tree was cut down. The original tree which gave the hill its name was replaced by a Californian Radiata Pine in the 1870’s.

The tree has been under attack in differing forms by Māori activists trying to make their political point known over the years. The only successful attack came in October 2000 by activist Mike Smith who used a chainsaw to try to remove the pine, however a chainsaw at 2am is easily heard and he was prevented from finishing his cut. The damage he had done was enough that the tree died and had to be removed by the local council.

The hill is also honoured in song, most famously by the Irish group U2 in their song One Tree Hill, written in honour of their Kiwi roadie Greg Carroll who was killed in a motorcycle accident while they were writing the Joshua Tree album. The song was only released in New Zealand and promptly went to number 1 on the music charts.

MOTAT (Museum of Transport and Technology)

The museum located a short bus trip from down town pays homage to all things mechanical that have a connection with New Zealand.

Spread over 2 locations joined by a restored Auckland tram line the grounds bracket the Auckland Zoo. As well as all the usual things you would expect to find (trucks, tractors, busses, steam engines, planes...)

MOTAT houses some very unique pieces; a replica of the first powered aircraft, built from the plans of the inventor Richard Pearce and arguably the first person to achieve a controlled powered flight,

Displays about aviation pioneers Jean Batten and the Walsh brothers, a Lancaster bomber and a replica of Sir Keith Parks (the defender of London in WW2) Hurricane fighter.

Kelly Tarltons

Kelly Tarltons Aquarium. Situated a short bus trip east of the downtown is the by-product of New Zealand's own diver/ adventurer Kelly Tarlton.

Kelly Tarltons dream was to bring the Southern Oceans life as viewed by divers to the everyday public with out getting their feet wet.

Converting a disused sewer treatment plant to a giant aquarium Kelly made his dream come true in 1985. Kelly died only a couple of months after its opening but he did get a glimpse of how successful it was to really become, as the same day he died the 10,000 visitors passed through its doors.

Here is a fantastic opportunity to see the ocean alive with out having to pass a dive certificate or don a wetsuit.

Gisborne

Things to do

Wainui Beach

Wainui Beach can have days where the surf is only for experienced surfers, and other days where beginners to intermediates can have fun out there too. If you’re an inexperienced swimmer, stick strictly to the patrolled area of the beach, clearly marked by flags — during summer, this stretch is patrolled by career lifeguards. Being east-facing, the beach also offers fantastic sunrise photos as the sun rises behind the breakers.

Cook's Memorial

History is often written by whoever holds the pen. Just as Columbus is credited with “discovering” America, despite the First Nations people who were already there, Captain Cook is remembered as the Englishman who claimed New Zealand for the Crown — with little regard, at the time, for the Māori who called it home. Encouragingly, the monument at Gisborne’s Cook Landing site has evolved with our understanding of that history. Alongside the original obelisk, it now features the Ikaroa sculpture, honouring the early Māori navigators Māia and Puhi-Kai-Iti, and nine pou remembering the Māori killed during Cook’s first, fraught encounter with local iwi. It’s a site that now tells both sides of the story.

Early Explorer Statues

Down by the port, where the Tūranganui River meets Waikanae Beach, three statues honour early figures connected to the region. The first, the Hawaiki Tūranga sculpture, represents Ruapani, a paramount chief with connections to all the local iwi of Tairāwhiti. Next along is Captain Cook, and a little further on, pointing out toward the headland that bears his name, is Nicholas Young — “Young Nick” — the Endeavour’s surgeon’s boy who first sighted the New Zealand coast.

Footrot Flats Statues

For any New Zealander born before the 2000s, “Footrot Flats” is just part of the vernacular. The strip follows a farm that only ever existed in the imagination of its creator, cartoonist Murray Ball. Ball found early recognition while based in the UK, with strips like “Stanley the Palaeolithic Hero” — the longest-running strip Punch magazine ever published. Returning to Gisborne, he bought a small farm, and from there created Footrot Flats. It ran in newspapers across New Zealand and Australia, was collected into more than 40 published books, and by the late ’80s had become a full-length animated feature film. Wal Footrot and his sheepdog, Dog, are the central characters, with much of the strip’s humour seen through Dog’s eyes. To get your photo with Wal and Dog, look for the bronze statue right beside the library in the middle of town. https://footrotflats.com/

Hastings

Things to do

Hawke's Bay Farmers' Market

If you’re in Hawke’s Bay and around Hastings on a Sunday, getting along to the farmers’ market is an absolute must. It’s one of the oldest and largest in the country, running since 2000, held in the tree-lined Waikoko Gardens at Tomoana Showgrounds. Pick up some fresh local produce, or maybe a craft piece that catches your eye to take home as a memento. Entry is free, running from 8:30am to 12:30pm every Sunday.

Hawke's Bay Wine Region

With over a hundred wineries making up the Hawke’s Bay wine region, not all of them have cellar doors, and not all cellar doors are open to the public. If you want to sample wines from the region, pop into the i-SITE, where you can pick up a free map showing which cellar doors are open, along with their hours and days.

Cape Kidnappers Gannet Colony

East of Hastings lies Cape Kidnappers, home to the world’s largest, most accessible mainland gannet colony — up to 20,000 birds nest here between September and April, before migrating to warmer climates. You can walk out to the colony yourself along the beach from Clifton, but the track is genuinely treacherous — it’s tide-dependent, and prone to slips that can take away sections of the path or bury it entirely. Your best option is one of the guided tours, whether by 4x4 or tractor. Not only do you get an experienced guide and their local knowledge, but they’ll take their own well-worn route to and from the colony, avoiding the worst of the hazards.

Bridge Pa Wine Triangle

Bridge Pa is a region within the Hawke’s Bay wine region, known as the Bridge Pa Triangle for the three roads that frame it. Along these three roads you’ll find eleven member wineries. You can reach the area by catching a public bus out and doing a cycle tour, or drive yourself — though driving raises the obvious problem: how does the driver get to sample the wine and still stay sober enough to get everyone home safely? A cycle tour, or arranging a designated driver, solves that one nicely.

Mangatainoka

Things to do

Tui Brewery

From 1889 until 2021, Tui Brewery was brewing beer at its Mangatainoka site. The iconic seven-storey building, built in 1931, was designed as a gravity-fed system — raw materials went in at the top, and bottled beer came out the bottom. When it was near completion, they discovered a major flaw: there was no lift or stairs to get to the top. The café, bar, and visitor centre now on site, known as Tui HQ, is a byproduct of Tui’s famously irreverent “Yeah Right” advertising campaign, launched in 1997. A long-running series of billboards would make a bold statement, then undercut it with the tagline “Yeah right.” You can pick up a book of some of the best billboards at the café.

Napier

Things to do

Art Deco Guided Walking Tour

Art Deco is a style of architecture designed to be strong, sturdy, and plain — and understanding what makes it unique is often far easier with a guided tour. Napier’s Art Deco Trust runs 1.5 to 2-hour guided walks through the city’s Art Deco Quarter, starting with a short film on the devastating 1931 earthquake that levelled Napier and led to its rebuild in this very style. You’ll see banks, fire stations, showplaces, and shopfronts along the way. What makes Napier’s Art Deco truly one-of-a-kind is the Kiwi twist: local architects wove Māori motifs into the design, blending zigzags and patterns from Māori art with the classic Art Deco style. Your guide will point these details out as you go — a fantastic way to explore an art style found nowhere else in quite the same form.

Art Deco Self-Guided Walking Tour

If you’d rather explore at your own pace, the Art Deco Trust also offers a self-guided walking map of Napier’s Art Deco Quarter. It’s a handy way to see the key buildings without booking a set tour time, though you’ll miss out on the stories and insider details a live guide brings — you’re really just following the map and reading as you go.

Norsewood

Things to do

NZ Natural Clothing

Norsewear was started by Ola Rian, a Norwegian army captain who fell in love with New Zealand on a skiing holiday, married a Kiwi woman, Shirley, and began making Scandinavian-style ski hats and socks from New Zealand wool as a side hustle back in 1963. That’s where the now-iconic “Farm Fleck” sock was born, and it’s been in continuous production ever since. While brands like Norsewear, Swanndri, and others have faced real competition in recent years from lighter synthetic materials, they’ve held onto their reputation thanks to the warmth, quality, and craftsmanship of their garments. The Norsewear factory itself sits right behind NZ Natural Clothing, though the two have no financial connection. Still, you’ll find Norsewear, Swanndri, and other iconic New Zealand woollen brands on the shelves inside — a quick stop into the shop often turns up bargains on quality wool garments. Who knows, you might even walk away with a pair of possum fur socks.

Pahiatua

Things to do

Harvard Adventure Park

In the middle of Pahiatua’s main street, you’ll find a World War Two American Harvard trainer, used by the New Zealand Air Force for many years. The original NZ918, which stood in the main street for decades, had to be taken down in 2015 due to rust and safety issues. It’s since been replaced with a fibreglass replica, now built with a children’s slide as part of it. It was installed to be a point of interest for Pahiatua, and that’s exactly what it became — many people know the town simply as “the plane in the main street.”

Things to do

Agrodome

Showcasing our agricultural New Zealand. The show has sheep on performance, sheep being shorn, dogs working all with good nature and banter.

Wingspan

This native raptor sanctuary is a jewel in the regions crown of activities.

Not only does Wingspan aid the recovery of sick and injured raptors for release they also have displays of falconry.

You will get to see New Zealand's fasted bird of prey the karearea (falcon) hunt a decoy, you also get to see these birds up close as they are introduced to you.

Whakarewarewa Geothermal Park and Cultural Experience

Whakarewarewa Geothermal park in the southern district of Rotorua is a geothermal park like the others in respect of its geothermal activity on show.

But the park also houses a carving school where young carvers come to learn traditional carving. Visitors can walk around and see the students at work or what they are working on.

At 12:30 daily there is a cultural show in front of the Wharenui.

Wai-o-tapu Geothermal Park

South of Rotorua is a Wai-o-tapu Geothermal Park.

It has on display all of the geothermal wonders, hot bubbling mud, sulphur laden pools of bright yellow shores, weird coloured pools and geysers.

It is home to the Lady Knox Geyser, the geyser is triggered daily at 10:15.

Redwood forest and Treetop walk

The Redwood Forest is a tree growing experiment when the future of New Zealand commercial forestry was being explored. While Radiata Pine was discovered to flourish and grow quickly in New Zealand and become the predominant species for commercial forests. this stand of Redwoods remains.

It is free to walk through the Redwood forest and enjoy the trees and is an enjoyable short walk.

At night however the treetop walk comes alive. The tress and David Trubridge art that hangs in the tress and from the walkway are illuminated. Providing a stunning spectacle.

Tamaki Tours

This Māori culture show is an experience not just a show. While you can drive, I suggest you take the bus pick up option. They will pick you up and take you to their village.

At the village you will be welcomed via a whero (challenge).

Once the challenge has been accepted you will look through what a Māori village would have looked like pre-European arrival. then to the pōwhiri (welcome) where you will be treated to song and dance, with lots of lighthearted humour.

Now you have been welcomed you move to the wharekai where a prepared buffet hangi (food cooked in an earth oven) has been prepared.

While this is a polished tourism operation it is a slice of traditional culture and well worth the money.

Wai ariki

Deluxe thermal spa. Wai Ariki is locally Māori owned and operated day spa. Offering a range of exquisite spa treatments.

While not the place for people wanting low budget options. If you are prepared to splash out and treat yourself, soaking for a few hours in the pools and enjoying a massage with native essential oils is a magical way to spend an afternoon.

Taupō

Things to do

Tongario Crossing

The Tongariro Crossing is a one day alpine crossing that transverses Mt Tongariro. It is billed as one of the best most accessible alpine crossings in the world.

Like all alpine activities it is very weather dependant as per its safety. You can drive yourself to the start.. but remember you will need to get from the end back to the start (its a one way trek).

I recommend for safety, comfort and ease sake to use a transport operator out of Taupō or Tūrangi. They will take you to the start and pick you up at the end. Also inform your safety concerns that may be relevant for that day.

This is an alpine trek, you will need to take, warm clothes as well as clothes for if you get hot. Quick drying attire and windproof over clothes are highly recommended. Take sunscreen and food (light and easy eating foods) with lots of water.

Rock n Ropes

Top 5

Rock n Ropes near Taupō, the cheapest thrill/adrenaline-pumping/action activity in the country. A high ropes adventure corse designed to test your nerve and ability to deal with heights. For around $80 for a half day course and with a fear factor far above bungie jumping this is bang for buck. The course is one of the safest activities of its type available yet the perceived danger is higher than it’s equivalentents.

Fishing Guides

Lake Taupo and the Tongariro River are trout fishing country — Turangi’s long worn the title of trout fishing capital of the world, and it’s not just talk. Whether you fancy trolling the lake from a chartered boat or working a fly rod along the Tongariro’s famous pools, there’s a guide for every style and skill level. Most trips supply all the gear, and many will even cook your catch for you on the way back in. You’ll need a Taupo fishing licence — this fishery runs its own separate system, not the usual Fish & Game one — sorted easily online or at any tackle shop. Pop into the Taupo i-SITE and they’ll match you up with a guide or charter to suit.

Taupo Tandem Skydive

Skydiving has become one of the must-do activities for anyone chasing adrenaline on their New Zealand adventure, and Taupo Tandem Skydiving is one of the original operators offering tourist jumps in the country. You’ll get sweeping views over Lake Taupo, the Tongariro volcanoes, and the Kaimanawa Ranges on the way down. My advice: pick up the photo and video package from your jump — worth every cent when you’re back home showing your mates. https://taupotandemskydiving.com/

DeBretts Spa

DeBretts Spa is a set of hot pools right in the town of Taupo, fed by natural geothermal water. Unlike the neighbouring pools in Rotorua, there’s no sulphurous smell or staining to the water here — just clean, mineral-rich warmth to soak in after a day on the road or the lake. A great way to unwind, whatever the weather. https://www.taupodebretts.co.nz/

Tokomaru Bay

Things to do

Tokomaru Bay Wharf

Like its southern neighbour at Tolaga Bay, Tokomaru Bay’s wharf was built to load ships with local produce at a time when roads were little more than mud tracks. What sets it apart is the surrounding infrastructure still standing nearby — the old freezing works, wool store, and shipping company offices that once made this a bustling industrial hub. The wharf and buildings are long since out of commercial use, but they make for a fascinating, atmospheric photo stop, half ghost town, half living history.

Tolaga Bay

Things to do

Tolaga Bay Wharf

The historic and iconic Tolaga Bay Wharf was built to load ships with local produce from the area, at a time when roads were little more than mud tracks. Completed in 1929, it stretches 660 metres out to sea, making it the longest wharf in the Southern Hemisphere. Long since discontinued for shipping, it now offers fantastic, unique photo opportunities and a nice walk out along the pier.

Tūrangi

Things to do

Tongario Crossing

The Tongariro Crossing is a one day alpine crossing that transverses Mt Tongariro. It is billed as one of the best most accessible alpine crossings in the world.

Like all alpine activities it is very weather dependant as per its safety. You can drive yourself to the start.. but remember you will need to get from the end back to the start (its a one way trek).

I recommend for safety, comfort and ease sake to use a transport operator out of Taupō or Tūrangi. They will take you to the start and pick you up at the end. Also inform your safety concerns that may be relevant for that day.

This is an alpine trek, you will need to take, warm clothes as well as clothes for if you get hot. Quick drying attire and windproof over clothes are highly recommended. Take sunscreen and food (light and easy eating foods) with lots of water.

Fishing Guides

Lake Taupo and the Tongariro River are trout fishing country — Turangi’s long worn the title of trout fishing capital of the world, and it’s not just talk. Whether you fancy trolling the lake from a chartered boat or working a fly rod along the Tongariro’s famous pools, there’s a guide for every style and skill level. Most trips supply all the gear, and many will even cook your catch for you on the way back in. You’ll need a Taupo fishing licence — this fishery runs its own separate system, not the usual Fish & Game one — sorted easily online or at any tackle shop. Pop into the Taupo i-SITE and they’ll match you up with a guide or charter to suit.

Waipoua Forest

Things to do

Tāne Mahuta

Nestled in the Waipoua Forest a short walk from the road is Tāne Mahuta.

The approximately 2000 year old Kauri tree having a volume of 516 cubic metres (18,000 cubic feet) is the largest tree by volume in the Southern Hemisphere.

His name translates to God of the Forest, referencing back to his role in separating Papatunuku (Earth mother) and Rangi Nui (Sky Father).

Due to the deadly disease "Kauri dieback" that as its name suggests is a fungal infection that often kills the Kauri tree, touching the tree is not allowed incase you have accidently transported the fungus in your travels.

Te Papa

Top 5

Te Papa National Museum, offers a fantastic overview of New Zealand’s history and culture. Has fantastic displays with as little or as much reading as you wish without loosing an understanding of the displays. It also houses some of the countries most treasured artefacts.

Whakaari/White Island Flights

Whakaari/White Island is New Zealand’s most active volcano, sitting out in the Bay of Plenty. Tragically, an eruption on 9 December 2019 claimed the lives of 22 people who were on the island at the time, and landing tours have not resumed since. You can still see the island up close on a scenic flight, with regular departures from Whakatāne, Tauranga, and Rotorua giving you a view of the crater and steam plumes from the air. https://whakaari-tours.com/

Woodville

Things to do

Te Ahu a Tūranga Marae

Finding examples of a stunning wharetupuna (ancestral meeting house) like Te Ahu a Tūranga Marae here in Woodville is rare — not because they’re uncommon, but because you don’t often see one sitting so close to the main road. For a passing tourist, take the opportunity: stop, stand at the fence, and admire the architecture, artwork, and craftsmanship that has gone into this building. Look closely and you’ll see that at the very top of the gable sits the tupuna, the ancestor the wharetupuna is named for and centred on — their arms extended in welcome, forming the two rafters of the roof, their body forming the building itself. Please be respectful when taking photos, do not enter the grounds, and take any rubbish with you.

Auckland

Auckland

New Zealand's largest city and the world's largest Polynesian city, built across 63 volcanic cones and two harbours. Capital until 1865, when Wellington took over. Home to Team New Zealand's America's Cup years (1995-2000) and the birthplace of Pacifica Hip-Hop in South Auckland. Worth knowing: this is New Zealand's business centre, not really 'New Zealand' itself — spend long enough to get yourself sorted then the rest of the country. Traffic peaks 6:30-9:30am and 3:30-6pm on weekdays.

Good to Know

Auckland: named by Governor William Hobson in 1840 after George Eden, Earl of Auckland. New Zealand's largest city, with around 1.4 million people spread across two harbours and 63 volcanic cones (dormant, not extinct). Capital of New Zealand from 1840 to 1865. World's largest Polynesian city, with strong Pacific and Māori cultural influence, including the genre of Pacifica Hip-Hop (OMC, Che Fu, King Kapisi) that emerged from South Auckland in the late 1990s. Held the America's Cup from 1995 until losing it to Switzerland's Alinghi in 2007.

Northland

Warkworth

Named after a Northumberland town by founder John Anderson Brown, who arrived as a saw miller in 1843 and founded the settlement in 1853. Began as a timber town, briefly hosted New Zealand's first Portland Cement Company from 1865, and is now a well-serviced rural service town for local farms. The satellite station on the hill above town, built in 1971, was once a critical link relaying phone calls and TV data to and from the world.

Good to Know

Warkworth: named after the English town in Northumberland, hometown of founder and saw miller John Anderson Brown, who established the settlement in 1853. Originally a timber town; shifted to cement production after high-grade limestone was discovered in 1865, home to New Zealand's first Portland Cement Company until it relocated to Whangārei in 1918. Site of the Warkworth Satellite Station (1971), a key early link in New Zealand's telecommunications with the world.

Northland

Matakohe

Home to one of the region's Kauri Museums — a worthwhile stop for its displays and its handy distance from Auckland, a couple of hours north. A good place to stretch your legs and learn about the kauri timber and gum industries that once defined Northland.

Good to Know

Matakohe: te reo Māori for the area where kohekohe trees grow (mata, headland; kohe, kohekohe). Home to the Kauri Museum, showcasing the region's kauri timber and gum industries. Kauri is New Zealand's largest native tree, growing up to 30m tall with girths to 23m; some specimens are estimated at 2,000 years old. Harvesting native kauri timber is now illegal.

Northland

Dargaville

A rural service town on the Kaipara Harbour, known as New Zealand's Kūmara Capital — producing around two-thirds of the country's sweet potato crop, after kauri-poisoned soil pushed farmers away from other options. Also strong dairy country, and the gateway to the ancient kauri of Waipoua Forest further north.

Good to Know

Dargaville: named after timber merchant Joseph McMullen Dargaville. A rural service town on the Kaipara Harbour. Kauri felling left soil too poisoned for easy farming, leading locals to kūmara (sweet potato) instead — Dargaville now produces around two-thirds of New Zealand's kūmara crop. Also a strong dairy region and gateway to the Waipoua Forest kauri stands.

Northland

Waipoua Forest

Home to New Zealand's largest kauri trees — Tāne Mahuta, the biggest living kauri by volume, and Te Matua Ngahere, the oldest at an estimated 2,000 years. The forest itself was saved from logging in 1952 by a 70,000-signature petition. Stick to the wooden walkway when visiting Tāne Mahuta — kauri roots are shallow and easily damaged by foot traffic.

Good to Know

Waipoua Forest: te reo Māori, likely wai (water) and poua (shellfish), referencing shellfish once gathered at nearby river mouths. The largest remaining stand of native forest in Northland, saved from logging in 1952 after a 70,000-signature petition; 15,000 hectares are now a protected reserve. Home to Tāne Mahuta (Lord of the Forest), New Zealand's largest living kauri at 51.2m tall and 13.7m girth, and Te Matua Ngahere (Father of the Forest), the oldest known kauri at an estimated 2,000 years.

Northland

Hokianga Harbour

Regarded by Ngāpuhi, New Zealand's largest iwi, as their ancestral point of origin — the explorer Kupe is said to have departed from here on his return voyage to Hawaiki. A West Coast Northland region built on forestry and dairy, and gateway to the kauri giants of Waipoua Forest further south.

Good to Know

Hokianga Harbour: te reo Māori for 'to return' — the place from which the explorer Kupe is said to have departed on his return voyage to Hawaiki. Considered the ancestral origin point of Ngāpuhi, New Zealand's largest iwi, after the Mataatua waka's Puhi sailed north from a dispute in Whakatāne. A West Coast Northland region built on forestry and dairy, and the southern gateway to the Waipoua kauri forest.

Northland

Opononi

A relaxed harbourside settlement, known for Opo — a wild dolphin who charmed visitors through the summer of 1955-56 before dying just days after being granted legal protection. Worth stopping at the information centre, which tells her story in a short film, and the foreshore monument in her memory.

Good to Know

Opononi: name possibly a misspelling of Opounoni, referencing a fishing pot used at a nearby stream mouth. A small settlement on the Hokianga Harbour, famous for Opo — a wild bottlenose dolphin who befriended locals and visitors over the 1955-56 summer before dying suddenly, days after being granted legal protection. A monument on the foreshore and a short film at the information centre tell her story.

Northland

Kaikohe

A quiet farming town whose unassuming main street hides deep Ngāpuhi history. Home ground of war chief Hongi Hika, who used early trading advantages with Europeans and two overseas trips to acquire muskets, then led a fierce campaign of utu across the North Island in the 1820s. None of that history is visible driving through — it lives in the stories rather than the streets.

Good to Know

Kaikohe: te reo Māori, kai (food) and kohe (tree/climbing plant); originally Kaikohekohe, after survivors of a raid sheltered among kohekohe trees. Heartland of Ngāpuhi and home to war chief Hongi Hika (1772-1828), who used trade connections and two trips abroad (Sydney and London) to acquire muskets, then led a devastating musket-armed campaign across the North Island in the 1820s driven by utu.

Northland

Kaitaia

Last stop for proper supplies before heading north to Cape Rēinga — stock up here if you're camping further on. New Zealand's northernmost and consistently warmest town, with a strong Dalmatian heritage dating from the 1880s kauri gum rush. Nearby Ahipara marks the southern end of Ninety Mile Beach, and Gum Diggers Park north of town turns recovered ancient kauri logs into furniture and art.

Good to Know

Kaitaia: te reo Māori, kai (food) and taia (thrown about) — from a legend of two women competing to store the most food. New Zealand's northernmost town and consistently its warmest, supporting avocado and grape growing. Home to a strong Dalmatian immigrant community dating from the 1880s kauri gum rush, with many local Māori able to trace Dalmatian ancestry. Last major shopping stop before Cape Rēinga — no fuel or supplies further north beyond a lone service station just south of town.

Northland

Cape Reinga

The northernmost point of State Highway 1 and one of the most spiritually significant sites in Māori culture — spirits are believed to leave from here via an 800-year-old pōhutukawa on the cliff face, on their journey back to Hawaiki. Stunning, stark scenery where the Tasman Sea meets the Pacific. Tapotupotu Bay just south has one of the most idyllic beachside campsites around, though currents are strong and the mosquitoes take no prisoners.

Good to Know

Cape Rēinga: te reo Māori for 'the underworld'. The spiritual departure point for Māori spirits after death, said to descend an 800-year-old pōhutukawa clinging to the cliff before leaping to Hawaiki. The northernmost point of State Highway 1, marked by an iconic lighthouse where the Tasman Sea meets the Pacific Ocean. Tapotupotu Bay, just south, offers a DOC-managed beachside campsite (basic facilities, small fee, strong currents).

Northland

Paihia (Bay of Islands)

Main hub for exploring the Bay of Islands, with most tour operators based in or booking through town — sea kayaking, sailing, jet boating, the works. No surf and no real currents here, so it's essentially a big calm swimming pool. Book well ahead for summer and Christmas, when accommodation fills up months in advance. A ferry across to Russell is easier than the long drive around.

Good to Know

Paihia: believed to come from pai (good, te reo Māori) plus 'here' (English) — it's good here. Main tourist hub of the Bay of Islands, with most regional tour operators based in or booking through town. Accommodation books out months ahead over summer and Christmas. A ferry connects to Russell (formerly Kororāreka) across the water.

Northland

Waitangi

Site of the Treaty of Waitangi, signed 6 February 1840 — New Zealand's founding document, marked each year on Waitangi Day. The Māori and English versions of the treaty differ on a critical point: sovereignty. That gap has shaped nearly two centuries of history since, and is still actively debated today. The grounds include the Treaty House and a working marae — a genuinely significant stop for understanding the country's history.

Good to Know

Waitangi: te reo Māori, wai (water) and tangi (to weep) — the weeping waters, from a nearby waterfall. Site of the Treaty of Waitangi, signed 6 February 1840 between the British Crown and Māori chiefs — New Zealand's founding document and the basis of Waitangi Day. The Māori and English versions of the Treaty differ significantly on the question of sovereignty, a discrepancy still debated and litigated today.

Northland

Whangarei

Northland's largest city and a good place to restock before heading further north. Nearly emptied out during Hōne Heke's 1840s campaign, but recovered to reach city status by the mid-1960s, helped along by New Zealand's only oil refinery at nearby Marsden Point. Whangārei Falls, a 24m drop a few minutes off the main road, is a peaceful stop — and in summer, a popular (if slightly alarming) local cliff-jumping spot.

Good to Know

Whangārei: te reo Māori, whanga (harbour) and rei (cherished possession), from a legend of a woman who waited too long for her lover's return. Largest city in Northland, on the Whangārei Harbour. Nearly emptied during Hōne Heke's campaign in the 1840s but recovered, reaching city status by the mid-1960s alongside the opening of New Zealand's only oil refinery at Marsden Point (1964). Whangārei Falls, a 24m waterfall a few minutes from the city centre, is a popular local swimming and cliff-jumping spot.

Northalnd

Wellsford

A tidy rural service town for local dairy farms, close enough to Auckland to support good cafés on the main road — worth a coffee stop if you left the city later in the morning. The name is entirely made up: an acronym of the surnames of its nine founding families.

Good to Know

Wellsford: an acronym of the surnames of its nine founding families — Watson, Edger, Lester, Levet, Simpson, Foster, Oldfield, Ramsbottom and Dibble. A rural service town for the local dairy farming community, close enough to Auckland to support a well-presented main street.

Waikato

Rangariri

Site of one of the most significant battles of the New Zealand Wars. In 1863 around 500 Māori defenders built a kilometre of hand-dug earthworks between the Waikato River and Lake Waikare to hold the line against Cameron's advancing colonial forces. After a day of fighting from three directions the defenders were surrounded and surrendered. The earthworks and a battle monument are visible on the western side of the road.

Good to Know

The Rangiriri area is significant in the New Zealand Wars. The Kingitanga movement (1850s) was a political response to European land acquisition, uniting iwi under a Māori King. Pōtatau Te Wherowhero became first Māori King in 1858, succeeded by Tāwhiao in 1860. Tāwhiao declared Waikato lands under his protection, leading to the Waikato War (1863–64). Rangiriri was the site of a major battle in that conflict.

Waikato

Huntly/Taupiri

Dominated by the twin cooling stacks of Huntly Power Station, New Zealand's largest thermal station, capable of supplying up to 20 percent of the country's electricity. Coal has been mined here since 1842, and the Waikato River running alongside is itself a major hydro producer, with 32 stations along its length.

Good to Know

Huntly is dominated by the Huntly Power Station, New Zealand's largest thermal power station (up to 1,448MW from coal and natural gas). It can supply up to 20 percent of New Zealand's electricity. The Waikato River supports 32 hydroelectric stations. Coal has been mined in the area since its discovery in 1842.

Waikato

Taupiri.

Look for the Taupiri urupā on the hillside to the right as you enter town — the principal Tainui burial ground, where position on the slope reflects rank. Dame Te Atairangikaahu, the Māori Queen who reigned 40 years, and comedian Billy T James are both buried here. Worth a quiet moment as you pass.

Good to Know

Taupiri: Te reo Māori — tau (ridge of a hill), piri (to keep close to). Home to the principal Tainui urupā (burial ground) on the adjacent hill. Rank is reflected in burial height on the hill. Notable burials include Dame Te Atairangikaahu (Māori Queen, 40-year reign) and comedian Billy T James.

Waikato

Ngaruawhaia

Seat of the Māori King and home of the Kingitanga movement. Tūrangawaewae Marae, easily seen from River Road, was built in 1920 under Te Puea Herangi and remains New Zealand's most significant marae. Parts of the town centre are laid out in the shape of the Union Flag — an irony not lost on locals.

Good to Know

Ngāruawāhia: Te reo Māori — ngā (the), rua (pit), whāhia (broken into) — food storage pits opened for guests. Seat of the Māori King and home of the Kingitanga movement. Tūrangawaewae Marae, built 1920 under Māori princess Te Puea Herangi during a Kingitanga resurgence, is New Zealand's most significant marae.

Waikato

Te Kowhai/Whatawhata

Two small towns on State Highway 39. Watch for a tight 35km/h corner just inside the Te Kowhai speed zone, followed by a school zone — it catches drivers out regularly. Whatawhata sits at the SH23/SH39 intersection and is usually pronounced locally as 'wot-a-wot-a'.

Good to Know

Te Kowhai: Te reo Māori — te (the), kowhai (native flowering tree). Drivers note: 35km/h corner immediately inside the 50km/h zone, followed by a school speed zone. Whatawhata: Te reo Māori — elevated food store. Commonly pronounced locally as 'wot-a-wot-a'. Sits at the SH 23 and SH 39 intersection.

Waikato

Pirongia

Sits below Mount Pirongia, a volcanic remnant at the heart of Pirongia National Park and home to the largest block of native bush left in the Waikato — walks for most fitness levels. The town was called Alexandra until 1964. In 1881, Māori King Tāwhiao laid down arms here on the corner of Crozier and Franklin Streets, ending armed conflict in the region and withdrawing into what became the King Country.

Good to Know

Pirongia: shortened from Pirongia-te-aroaro-o-Kahu (the health restoring purification of Kahuere). Mt Pirongia, a volcanic cone visible from SH 39, lies within Pirongia National Park — home to the largest remaining native bush block in the Waikato. The area was historically abandoned by Māori due to intense inter-tribal conflict; Europeans later built a redoubt. Walking tracks of varying difficulty available.

Hamilton

Major economic centre for the Waikato. The city is home to the University of Waikato and Wintec (Waikato Institute of Technology).

Good to Know

Hamilton: major economic centre for the Waikato region. Home to the University of Waikato and Wintec (Waikato Institute of Technology).

Waikato

Taupó

Sits on Lake Taupō, a caldera formed by the largest volcanic eruption in recorded human history (181 AD) — still the most active rhyolite volcano on Earth. Trout fishing built the town's original reputation; skydiving, high ropes, and geothermal pools without Rotorua's sulphur smell built its modern one. Three competing drop zones keep skydive prices among the lowest in the country.

Good to Know

Taupō: abbreviated from Taupō nui a Tia — the great shoulder cloak of Tia (the lake's discoverer). Lake Taupō is New Zealand's largest lake, in a supervolcanic caldera. The Taupō eruption approximately 1,800 years ago was one of the most violent in recorded human history. The Waikato River (New Zealand's longest) begins here. Major tourism centre for fishing, adventure activities, and volcanic plateau access.

Waikato

Wairakei

Home to Huka Falls — only 11 metres high but shifting up to 270 cubic metres of water a second through a narrow gorge, reachable by an easy 45-minute riverside walk from Taupō. Also holds Craters of the Moon (a steaming geothermal field created as a side effect of NZ's first geothermal power station) and the Aratiatia control gates, which release the Waikato River into dramatic rapids daily at 10am and 2pm (plus 4pm in summer).

Good to Know

Wairakei Geothermal Park, just north of Taupō, contains New Zealand's first geothermal power station. Huka Falls (huka — foaming) is 11 metres high but carries 32–270 cubic metres of water per second through a narrow channel. Free access via a 45-minute riverside walk from Taupō or by road to the carpark.

Waikato

Turangi

Small fishing and forestry town — worth a stop for trout fishing or as a cheaper base for the Tongariro Crossing, but Taupō is generally the better overnight option otherwise. SH1 continues south through the Desert Road, a stark, wind-scoured volcanic plateau that gets plenty of rain despite its name — passes an Army training area (heed the signs) and the Rangipo Prison Farm.

Good to Know

Tūrangi: named after a Māori chief — tu (to stand), rangi (sky). Small fishing, tourism, and forestry town. SH 1 continues south through the Three Sisters and Desert Road, a high-altitude volcanic plateau route. Rangipo Prison Farm is passed along this route. Taupō is generally a better base unless you are a serious trout angler.

Waikato

Waiouru

Home to New Zealand's main Army base and the National Army Museum — a genuinely moving stop even for the non-military-minded, marking the country's heaviest-ever casualty rate (one in eight) during WWII. West toward Ohakune, the road crosses the Whangaehu River at Tangiwai, site of New Zealand's worst rail disaster — 151 died on Christmas Eve 1953 when a lahar from Ruapehu destroyed the bridge moments before an express train arrived.

Good to Know

Waiouru: Te reo Māori — wai (water), o (of), uru (west) — the western arm of the Hautupu River. Originally a sheep farming settlement, slow development from 1904 (post office, accommodation) then rail in 1907. Army established 1936; now New Zealand's primary Army base. The New Zealand Army Museum is a recommended visit.

Waikato

Ohakune

New Zealand's main carrot-growing area — hard to miss the giant carrot marking the town — and the closest base to Tūroa ski field on Ruapehu's southern slopes. Tūroa and Whakapapa together form the country's largest ski area, with good accommodation and gear hire available in town.

Good to Know

Ohakune: Te reo Māori — O (place of), hakune (to be careful). New Zealand's primary carrot-growing area and closest base to Tūroa ski field (southern Ruapehu slopes). Tūroa and Whakapapa together form New Zealand's largest ski area. A giant carrot sculpture marks the town. Good accommodation and ski hire available.

Waikato

Tongariro National Park

New Zealand's first national park, gifted to the nation in 1887 by Ngāti Tūwharetoa chief Horonuku Te Heuheu. Home to Ruapehu and Ngāuruhoe, and to the Tongariro Alpine Crossing — one of the world's best-known one-day hikes through volcanic terrain so otherworldly it helped double as Middle Earth in Lord of the Rings. Use a local shuttle operator rather than your own vehicle; mountain weather turns fast and the crossing closes for long stretches in winter.

Good to Know

Tongariro National Park was established in 1887 when Ngāti Tūwharetoa chief Horonuku Te Heuheu gifted the land to the nation — New Zealand's first national park and fourth in the world. Contains Ruapehu (2,940m) and Ngāuruhoe (2,410m). The Tongariro Alpine Crossing is one of the world's most recognised one-day walks. Dual UNESCO World Heritage status for natural and cultural values.

Waikato

Paeroa

Birthplace of L&P (Lemon & Paeroa), New Zealand's own soft drink, first bottled from a local mineral spring in 1908. Production moved to Auckland decades ago and it's now Coca-Cola-owned, but the giant L&P bottle on the main street remains a mandatory photo stop.

Good to Know

Paeroa: Te reo Māori — pae (mountain range), roa (long) — referring to the range behind town. Birthplace of Lemon & Paeroa (L&P), one of New Zealand's most iconic soft drinks. In 1908 the natural mineral spring water was first commercially bottled with lemon. The giant L&P bottle on the main street is a popular landmark.

Waikato

Matamata

Farming town on the flat, fertile Hauraki Plains — but best known as the real-world Hobbiton, filmed on a local farm for the Lord of the Rings and Hobbit trilogies. Tours take in the earthworks and hobbit-hole doors; most of the polish was added digitally, so it's more modest in person than on screen, but the rolling green countryside is unmistakable. Also strong horse-racing and stud country.

Good to Know

Matamata: Te reo Māori — matamata (headland), originally referring to a pā above a swamp. Internationally known as the filming location for Hobbiton from Peter Jackson's Lord of the Rings and Hobbit trilogies. Guided tours of the farm set's earthworks and hobbit hole doors are available.

King Country

Otorohanga

Grew from an 1886 railway construction camp — the line had stalled here for years while Māori chiefs refused it further access through the King Country. Makes a good, well-serviced alternative base if accommodation near the Waitomo Caves is booked out.

Good to Know

Otorohanga: Te reo Māori — O (place of), torohanga (food for a journey). A chief stretched meagre supplies the full distance to Taupo using karikea (resourcefulness). Founded 1886 as a railway construction camp; progress south was delayed when Māori chiefs including Rewi Maniapoto refused rail access. Known for the Kiwiana Walk and proximity to the Waitomo Caves.

King Country

Waitomo

One of New Zealand's premier tourist draws, with tourism dating back to 1900. Options range from the classic glowworm cave boat trip — a couple of hours, real highlight territory — through to Black Water Rafting (inner-tubing an underground river) and the seven-hour Lost World abseil-and-cave adventure. Caves were returned to local Māori management in 1989 after a period of Government administration.

Good to Know

Waitomo: Te reo Māori — wai (water), tomo (hole in the ground), reflecting the water-filled limestone caves. Tourism since early 1900s, originally led by Māori guides. Government took over administration due to vandalism; management returned to Tainui people in 1989. Famous for the endemic New Zealand glowworm (Arachnocampa luminosa). Activities include glowworm cave tours, blackwater rafting, and abseiling.

King Country

Te Kuiti

Take the main street rather than staying on the highway — a sheep shearer statue, a Colin Meads statue, and a wharetupuna worth the short walk are all along it. Known as the shearing capital of the world; local legend David Fagan won the Golden Shears fifteen times. Around Easter the town hosts the NZ Shearing Championships, opening with several hundred to a couple of thousand sheep driven down the main street.

Good to Know

Te Kuiti: Te reo Māori — te (the), Kuiti (from Kuitianga, to compact) — reflecting compression of Māori into the King Country through land confiscation. Grew from a railway construction camp. Now promotes itself as the shearing capital of the world with annual shearing competitions.

King Country

Piopio

A traditional stop-off point for travellers between the King Country and Taranaki. The café and public toilets here are popular enough to get busy at peak times — worth timing your break around that if you can.

Good to Know

Piopio: te reo Māori for a native thrush (now extinct). A small King Country town that has long served as a rest stop for travellers heading to and from Taranaki — the café and public toilets can get busy at peak times.

King Country

Awakino

A blink-and-you'll-miss-it settlement — pub, a few houses, a service station where the gorge road meets the coast. The road south to Mokau is spectacular, with Taranaki visible on a clear day, but pull well off the highway for photos or wait for the proper rest area ten kilometres on.

Good to Know

Awakino: Te reo Māori — awa (river), kino (bad), referring to the muddy silted river. A tiny settlement at the junction of the Awakino Gorge road and the Tasman Sea. The gorge itself is a 15–20 minute scenic drive through limestone terrain on a narrow road with limited passing opportunities.

King Country

Mokau

A Taranaki town in all but province, with the local rugby club playing across the border and New Plymouth an hour away against Hamilton's three. Popular whitebait fishing base. A rest area on the hill south of town gives clear coastal views and a first proper look at the White Cliffs.

Good to Know

Mokau: named after a chief who slept here. Though technically in Waikato province, the community identifies with Taranaki — New Plymouth is an hour away, Hamilton three. The Mokau River marks the traditional provincial boundary. The town is a popular whitebait fishing base.

Taranaki

Uruti

A small farming valley twice used by the film industry — Vincent Ward's Vigil, and later The Last Samurai, with the production farm still offering bookable tours of the remaining village set.

Good to Know

Uruti: Te reo Māori — uru (grove), ti (cabbage tree). A small rural community and setting for two notable films: Vigil (directed by Vincent Ward) and The Last Samurai (starring Tom Cruise). The farm used for The Last Samurai retains remnants of the village set and offers tours.

Taranaki

Motunui

Home to the Methanex methanol plant, born out of Muldoon's Think Big programme and the world's first natural gas to synthetic gasoline plant when it opened in 1986. Now runs flexibly with global methanol demand, exporting most of its output to Japan, China and Korea.

Good to Know

Motunui: Te reo Māori — motu (island), nui (big). The Methanex Methanol Plant, built as part of PM Robert Muldoon's Think Big energy programme, opened 1986 as the world's first natural gas to synthetic gasoline plant. Since 2004 the Motunui and Waitara Valley sites operate flexibly in response to global methanol demand.

Taranaki

Waitara

Long overshadowed by New Plymouth next door. The town's freezing works, Swanndri factory and Subaru plant all closed through the 1980s-90s downturn, and most residents now commute to New Plymouth for work. Swanndri itself has deep local roots — bought by Waitara businessman John McKendrick in 1952 and grown from there, though it's now made in Timaru.

Good to Know

Waitara: Te reo Māori — wai (water), tara (from taranga, wide steps) — referring to chief Turi crossing the river in large strides. Māori displaced from New Plymouth during the Taranaki land wars settled the Waitara valley. Economy long centred on freezing works (meat processing), many of which closed during the farming industry crisis of the mid-1980s.

New Plymouth

A city that punches above its weight for festivals and art. The Rhododendron and Fringe Garden Festivals hit in late October, Pukekura Park's Festival of Lights runs December to January, and WOMAD brings world music to the Bowl of Brooklands. The Govett-Brewster Gallery holds a dedicated wing for kinetic artist Len Lye, whose Wind Wand sculpture moves on the coastal walkway. That walkway runs seven kilometres along a coast with genuinely world-class surf breaks — Taranaki's surf culture is real, not marketing.

Good to Know

New Plymouth was named after the Plymouth Company, a subsidiary of the NZ Company established by brothers Colonel William Wakefield and Captain Arthur Wakefield to colonise New Zealand. Wellington, Nelson, and New Plymouth all have Wakefield origins. Today known for the backdrop of Mt Taranaki and the Govett-Brewster Art Gallery.

Taranaki

Surf Highway 45

A scenic loop route around the western and southern flanks of Mount Taranaki, built to draw visitors off SH3 and past the surf beaches. Watch for the distinctive rounded hills south of Pihama — old lava flows from Taranaki's last eruption. Turn off at Bailey Road for a short stretch that actually hugs the coast.

Good to Know

Surf Highway 45 is a regional marketing route around the western and southern flanks of Mt Taranaki, connecting surf beaches and directing visitors around the mountain rather than via SH 3. To follow the actual coastline, turn seaward at Bailey Road — Coast Road runs a few kilometres along the rocky shore before rejoining the highway via Pungarehu Road.

Taranaki

Parihaka

A living Māori village and one of New Zealand's most significant historical sites. Under chiefs Te Whiti and Tohu the community responded to land confiscation with organised nonviolent resistance — pulling survey pegs, removing fences — until over 1,500 armed constabulary marched on the village in 1881, arresting the leaders and looting the settlement. Visitors can pay respects at the monuments, but it remains a real community — move quietly.

Good to Know

Parihaka: historic Māori settlement led by chiefs Te Whiti-o-Rongomai and Tōhū Kākahi. Initially welcoming to settlers, the leaders shifted to nonviolent resistance as Crown land confiscation became clear — sending ploughmen and weavers to obstruct. On 1 November 1881 over 1,500 armed personnel marched on the village. Te Whiti and Tōhū were arrested and the village looted. A defining symbol of nonviolent resistance in New Zealand history.

Hawera

Stratford

Stratford (full name Stratford upon Patea): named in tribute to Stratford-upon-Avon, England, birthplace of Shakespeare. Streets carry Shakespearian names. Patea

Patea: full name Patea nui a Turi. A concrete Māori waka on the main street honours explorer Turi, after whom many landmarks between Kawhia and Patea are named. Turi concluded his overland journey here. Europeans built a meat processing plant and shipping port; the port has since closed.

Whanganui

Good to Know

Whanganui: Te reo Māori — whanga (harbour), nui (big). The city gained national attention through former mayor Michael Laws' gang patch ban and a prolonged spelling debate (Whanganui vs Wanganui), resolved in favour of the te reo spelling. Known for arts, Victorian architecture, and the Whanganui River — in 2017 the first river in the world to be granted legal personhood.

Palmerston North: major economic centre for the Manawatu region. Home campus of Massey University, New Zealand's largest university, which also has campuses in Auckland and Wellington.

Otaki

Wellington

Wellington

New Zealand's capital, built on three active fault lines and shaken daily by mostly-unfelt tremors. The 1855 quake lifted the harbour foreshore so far that Lambton Quay — once the actual shoreline — now sits three blocks from the water. Don't rush Te Papa; budget most of a day. Wellywood earned its nickname through Weta Workshop and the Lord of the Rings trilogy — the Embassy Theatre hosted the Return of the King premiere. For views, Mount Victoria; for wildlife, Zealandia's predator-fenced valley ten minutes from the CBD; for politics, the parliamentary precinct spanning a century of architecture from the wooden 1897 library to the 1981 Beehive.

Good to Know

Wellington: named after Arthur Wellesley, first Duke of Wellington. New Zealand's capital and second largest city. Sits on three major active fault lines with daily minor earthquakes. Known as Wellywood for its film industry (particularly Weta Workshop). Consistently ranked among the world's most liveable cities for its arts, café culture, and compact waterfront. Population approximately 450,000.

The Hutt Valley is named after Sir William Hutt, British MP and NZ Company chairman. Contains two cities: Lower Hutt (closer to Wellington Harbour, light industry and suburban) and Upper Hutt (residential, inland). Both are affordable commuter suburbs of Wellington with good rail connections.

Hawkes Bay

Hastings

Nearly named Hicksville, saved by fashion for naming towns after British colonial figures. Grew past neighbour Napier on the back of the fertile Heretaunga Plains and the rise of Wattie's, once New Zealand's largest food processor. Shares Napier's 1931 earthquake history and its own strong Art Deco streetscape, but escaped the fire that devastated Napier afterward. Also the hometown of novelist Alan Duff, whose Once Were Warriors drew directly on the city's harder side.

Good to Know

Hastings narrowly avoided being named Hicksville (after Francis Hicks, first to subdivide 1870s). Renamed after Warren Hastings, India's first Governor-General. Since WWII grown to become the economic centre of Hawke's Bay, New Zealand's primary fruit-growing region (apples, grapes, stone fruit, vegetables).

Hawkes Bay

Napier

Rebuilt almost entirely in Art Deco style after the 1931 earthquake killed 161 people and levelled much of the city — the result is the highest concentration of Art Deco buildings per capita anywhere in the world. Pick up the Art Deco walking guide from the i-SITE, or time a visit for the February festival. Also a major wine region, home to the National Aquarium, and the start of the spectacular but demanding Napier–Taupō Highway inland.

Good to Know

Napier was named after Sir Charles Napier, British military leader at the Battle of Meeanee. On 3 February 1931 a magnitude 7.8 earthquake killed 256 and destroyed much of the city. Reconstruction in Art Deco style created one of the world's finest concentrations of Art Deco architecture. The annual Art Deco Festival is a major international event.

Bay of Plenty

Rotorua

New Zealand's geothermal capital and its main centre for Māori cultural tourism — expect a lingering sulphur smell most visitors stop noticing after a day or two. Each geothermal park (Wai-o-tapu, Whakarewarewa, Hells Gate) offers the same core sights with its own point of difference, so pick one rather than doing them all. Also home to zorbing (a Kiwi invention), the Agrodome farm show, and the Buried Village, telling the story of the 1886 Tarawera eruption that destroyed the famous Pink and White Terraces.

Good to Know

Rotorua: Te reo Māori — roto (lake), rua (two) — the second lake discovered in the area. The city sits within New Zealand's major volcanic zone, producing extensive geothermal activity (hot pools, mud pools, geysers, steam vents). Māori have used geothermal features for cooking and bathing for centuries. The primary centre of Māori cultural tourism in New Zealand: performances, hangi, and carved meeting houses of the Te Arawa people.

Bay of Plenty

Wai-o-tapu

One of the region's larger geothermal parks, with mud pools, steam vents and vividly coloured sulphuric pools. Centrepiece is the Lady Knox Geyser, coaxed into erupting on schedule every morning at 10:30am with a shot of soap to break the water's surface tension — the eruption itself is entirely natural, just nudged onto a timetable.

Good to Know

Wai-o-tapu: Te reo Māori — wai (water), o (place of), tapu (sacred). A major geothermal park with geysers, mud pools, steam vents, and vivid sulphuric pools. The Lady Knox Geyser erupts daily at 10:30 am with the aid of soap to disrupt surface tension. Entry fee applies.

Bay of Plenty

Te Puke

Kiwifruit Capital of the World — the fruit itself is Chinese in origin, renamed from 'Chinese Gooseberry' to 'Kiwifruit' in the 1950s by exporters trying to win over American buyers. Industry grew from around 100 tonnes of exports in the 1970s to a $539 million annual business today. Worth the short detour east to Maketū, believed to be where the Arawa and Tainui people first sighted land after their voyage from Hawaiki.

Good to Know

Te Puke: Te reo Māori — te (the), puke (hill). Kiwifruit Capital of the World — the world's largest kiwifruit exporting region. Kiwifruit originated in China (yang-táo) and were propagated in New Zealand by Whanganui nurseryman Alexander Allison. New Zealand growers rebranded the fruit from Chinese gooseberry to Kiwifruit in the 1950s.

Tauranga is the commercial centre and New Zealand's busiest port. One of New Zealand's fastest-growing regions.

Bay of Plenty

Waihi

Boundary town between the Bay of Plenty and the Coromandel — orchard country meets gold country. Home to the Martha Mine, still producing around 100,000 ounces of gold and 750,000 ounces of silver a year across two operations. In 1912, a bitter miners' strike here turned violent on 'Black Tuesday', when unionist Fred Evans was beaten and later died — the only person to die as a direct result of industrial action in New Zealand history.

Good to Know

Waihi: Te reo Māori — wai (water), hi (to gush forth). Junction of Bay of Plenty and Coromandel. Economy defined by gold and silver mining; the Martha Mine is one of New Zealand's richest deposits and remains active. November 1912 saw a significant labour dispute between miners, unions, and mining companies, an important chapter in NZ labour history.

Karangahake Gorge: Te reo Māori — karanga (ridge), hake (hunchback) — the local cluster of hills. DOC reserve with short tracks through historic gold mining remains. Milford makes a full-day trip from Te Anau; take a bus tour rather than self-driving, since the road demands full attention and a scenic flight back is a good option. Doubtful Sound is better done as an overnight trip via Lake Manapouri. The Milford Track (4 days, book up to a year ahead through Ultimate Hikes) is New Zealand's most famous walk. Home to the takahē, rediscovered here in 1948 after 50 years presumed extinct, and to the kākāpō recovery search.

Good to Know

Fiordland National Park is New Zealand's largest (1,251,924 hectares) and one of the world's largest. The fiords were carved by glaciers during the last ice age (10,000–15,000 years ago); rising sea levels flooded the valleys to create the inlets. Milford Sound is technically a fiord, not a sound. UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Southland

Te Anau

On New Zealand's second largest lake, with three fiord-like arms reaching into the Murchison Mountains. Main base for Milford Sound, Doubtful Sound, and the Milford Track. Before heading into the park, the 32-minute Ata Whenua — Shadowlands film at the local cinema is a genuinely good introduction, filmed over five years by helicopter, jet boat and on foot.

Good to Know

Te Anau: Te reo Māori — disputed; most likely named after a chief's daughter. Other meanings: lake of many arms, water current in cave, to move as reeds. A town of approximately 1,800 on New Zealand's second largest lake (344km², 417m deep). Three fiord-like arms extend west into the Murchison Mountains. Primary base for Milford Sound and Doubtful Sound trips.

Southland

Manapouri

One of New Zealand's most beautiful lakes, and the unlikely site of the country's largest hydroelectric station. A 1960s plan to raise the lake 11 metres for power generation sparked one of New Zealand's first major environmental protests — the redesign that followed instead tunnels water 213 metres down through the mountains to turbines hidden in solid rock, entirely invisible from the lake surface. Most of the power feeds the Tiwai Point aluminium smelter. Small township (population ~240) and starting point for Doubtful Sound trips.

Good to Know

Manapouri: shortened from Manawapopore — manawa (heart), popore (sorrowful). Original name Moturau (many islands). Considered one of New Zealand's most beautiful lakes. Beneath the western mountains lies New Zealand's largest hydroelectric power station, powering the Tiwai Point aluminium smelter. 1960s plans to raise the lake level sparked one of New Zealand's first major environmental protest campaigns.

Southland

Doubtful Sound

Named by Cook in 1769, who wasn't confident he could sail back out against the wind and gave the fiord a wide berth. Quieter and more remote than Milford — access is by ferry across Lake Manapouri and bus down a narrow gravel road to Deep Cove, deliberately restricted, which keeps crowds down. Weather changes fast; four seasons in a day is a realistic expectation, not an exaggeration.

Good to Know

Doubtful Sound: named by Captain Cook in 1769 when he passed the entrance but was uncertain he could get winds to exit — he described it as 'a very snug harbour.' Less visited than Milford Sound and considered by many more dramatic. Access via ferry across Lake Manapouri and bus to Deep Cove. Day trips and overnight cruises operate from Deep Cove wharf.

Southland

Lumsden

Once the central rail junction for the whole southern region (1878-1979), with four lines radiating to Invercargill, Gore, Mossburn and Kingston. The old railway station is now the information centre — rail is long gone, and today the town is simply a useful road junction heading south.

Good to Know

Lumsden: originally named Elbow (bend in the Ōreti River), renamed after former Invercargill mayor George Lumsden. A historic rail junction from 1878 to 1979, with four lines serving Invercargill, Gore, Mossburn, and Kingston. The former railway station is now the information centre.

Southland

Winton

Commercial centre of a wealthy farming district, but better known as the home of Minnie Dean, the only woman ever hanged in New Zealand. Convicted of infanticide after running an informal baby-adoption business from her home, she was executed in 1895 and is buried in Winton — her grave went unmarked until 2009.

Good to Know

Winton: named after Thomas Winton, who helped survey the town in 1862. Commercial centre of a wealthy farming district. Also known as the home of Minnie Dean (Williamina Dean), the first and only woman hanged in New Zealand (1895). A Scottish immigrant who operated as a baby farmer before being convicted of murder.

Southland

Invercargill

New Zealand's southernmost city, founded in 1856 to import Australian livestock through the port at Bluff. Home to New Zealand's only indoor velodrome and its only fees-free polytechnic (SIT). Nearby Bluff has the famous Bluff oyster and marks the southernmost point of mainland New Zealand — the windswept signpost there is one of the country's most photographed spots. Also home town of Burt Munro, who spent 20-plus years in his shed converting a 1920 Indian motorcycle into a 288km/h land-speed record holder at age 68 — the story behind The World's Fastest Indian.

Good to Know

Invercargill: Scottish Gaelic inver (river mouth) + Cargill (Captain William Cargill, Dunedin co-founder). Established 1856 to provide a southern port for importing Australian livestock. New Zealand's southernmost city on a wide grid across fertile Southland plains. Notable for Scottish heritage, Bluff oysters, and Burt Munro (world speed record on a 1920 Indian motorcycle, celebrated in The World's Fastest Indian).

Southland

Stewart Island/Rakiura

New Zealand's southernmost main island, reached by ferry from Bluff or light aircraft from Invercargill. Most of the island is DOC reserve and one of the best places in the country to see a kiwi in the wild — ask at the Oban information centre about tours. Ulva Island, a short ferry and walk away, has been predator-free since 1997 and the birdsong shows it.

Good to Know

Stewart Island/Rakiura: named after William Stewart, first officer of the sealing ship Pegasus (1809). Rakiura: Te reo Māori — raki (sky), ura (glowing) — the island's exceptional sunsets and frequent Southern Lights. Small permanent population in Oban. Most of the island is DOC reserve, recognised as one of the best places in NZ to encounter native wildlife.

Southland

Catlins Forest Coast

A slow, scenic coastal route from Invercargill to Balclutha through native forest and rugged coastline — Nugget Point, Cathedral Caves, Slope Point, waterfalls and big cold surf breaks. Worth doing properly with an overnight stop if you have the time; if you're pressed for time, take SH1 through Gore instead, since the narrow, twisting Catlins road punishes rushing.

Good to Know

The Catlins: a coastal route from Invercargill to Balclutha through Catlins Forest along the rugged southeast coast. Notable features: Nugget Point Lighthouse, Cathedral Caves, Slope Point (southernmost South Island mainland point), waterfalls, sea stacks, and beaches. Wildlife: NZ sea lions, fur seals, yellow-eyed penguins, Hector's dolphins. Best explored slowly with overnight camping stops.

Southland

Milford Sound (self drive day trip)

This can be a challenging road to drive, especially if you are wanting too see the landscape as you drive. While there is many rewarding views, it is a road that has many near and not so near misses as drivers are distracted by what is around them.

I recommend taking a bus from either Queenstown or Te Anau.

If you do choose to drive yourself, allow a minimum of three hours' driving from Te Anau, and be aware of the many tourbuses that run the route especially aiming to arrive for the early afternoon sailings at the fiord. Winter driving conditions can be treacherous so pay close attention to road warnings and signage.

Good to Know

Milford Sound receives approximately seven metres of rain annually. Allow a minimum of three hours driving from Te Anau. Obey all road signage strictly — Road Closed in winter is enforced. Be aware of tour bus traffic with ferry deadlines. Permanent waterfalls: Lady Bowen and Stirling Falls. After heavy rain, hundreds of temporary waterfalls appear. Kipling called it the eighth wonder of the world.

Otago

Balclutha

Economic hub for rural southern Otago, home to Fonterra and Silver Fern Farms processing plants. Northern gateway to the Catlins Coast. The heritage-listed five-arch bridge over the Clutha River, built 1933, is the defining local landmark.

Good to Know

Balclutha: Scottish Gaelic — town on the Clyde (correctly Baile Chluaidh). Economic hub for southern Otago with Fonterra and Silver Fern Farms processing sites. The five-arched heritage bridge (244m, built 1933) carrying SH 1 over the Clutha River is the town's landmark. Northern gateway to the Catlins Coast.

Otago

Dunedin

A city built on Scottish identity — named after Edinburgh's old Gaelic name — with a strong University of Otago student population (locally 'Scarfies') and grey stone architecture from its gold rush and Victorian heyday. Highlights: Baldwin Street, the second-steepest street in the world and site of the annual Cadbury Jaffa Race; a Cadbury World tour, the only one outside the UK; and the Otago Peninsula, home to the only mainland royal albatross colony in the world plus yellow-eyed penguins and NZ sea lions. Larnach Castle is better described as a grand Victorian house — no moat, no walls, but worth the visit anyway.

Good to Know

Dunedin: Scottish Gaelic ancient name for Edinburgh. Named 1845 by Scottish founders who considered New Edinburgh. Grey stone architecture reflects gold rush and Scottish heritage. University of Otago (New Zealand's oldest university) gives strong academic character. The Otago Peninsula hosts the world's only mainland royal albatross colony, yellow-eyed penguins, and NZ sea lions.

Otago

Moeraki

Home to the Moeraki Boulders — 60-million-year-old limestone concretions up to 2 metres across, scattered on Koekohe Beach as the softer surrounding mudstone erodes away. Often mistaken for fossilised dinosaur droppings; they're not. Please don't climb on them — they're slowly eroding themselves. The beachside café is a good stop for fresh local seafood.

Good to Know

Moeraki: Te reo Māori — moe (to sleep), raki (day or sky) — to sleep during the day. Famous for the Moeraki Boulders — 60-million-year-old limestone concretions up to 2 metres in diameter on Koekohe Beach. Formed on the ancient sea floor as calcium carbonate accumulated around organic fragments. Surrounding mudstone has eroded to expose them. Short walk from roadside carpark.

Otago

Oamaru

A town of quiet surprises: a blue penguin colony (night tours are best, timed to the birds returning at dusk), a Victorian limestone precinct built from locally quarried 'Oamaru Stone' (soft enough to saw when first cut, hardening once exposed to air), and the Totara Estate, source of the sheep on board the Dunedin's 1882 voyage — the first successful refrigerated shipment of frozen meat from New Zealand to London, and a turning point for the whole agricultural economy.

Good to Know

Oamaru: Te reo Māori — O (place of), maru (an unnamed person). A quiet coastal town with a blue penguin colony (Eudyptula minor). Day and night tours available; evening tours recommended as birds return at dusk. Tour structured around an active monitoring programme. Victorian limestone precinct and steampunk culture add distinctive character.

Otago

Waitaki Valley

Defined by the Waitaki hydroelectric scheme — three major dams (Waitaki, Aviemore, Benmore) plus five smaller ones producing nearly 8,000 gigawatt hours between them. Benmore is New Zealand's largest earth-filled dam and second-largest power station after Manapouri. Near Duntroon, look for early 19th-century Māori rock art marking the seasonal route South Island Māori once followed between the coasts. Worth the detour rather than shortcutting up the coast — the Mackenzie Country scenery ahead more than repays the extra time.

Good to Know

Waitaki Valley: Te reo Māori — wai (water), taki (noisy) — the river in full flood moved boulders audibly before it was visible. The valley is defined by the Waitaki hydroelectric scheme. Surrounding landscape of braided flats, tussock hills, and Southern Alps peaks is spectacular.

Otago

Omarama

A flat, mountain-ringed basin that produces near-perfect gliding conditions — hosted the 2007 World Grand Prix Gliding Championships. Tandem flights are available through the local club if you want to ride the thermals yourself.

Good to Know

Ōmārama: Te reo Māori — O (place of), marama (light) — moonlight reflecting off the alps created the illusion of light at the valley's end. The flat mountain-ringed basin produces excellent thermals for gliding. World Grand Prix Gliding Championship held here 2007. Tandem glider flights available.

Canterbury

Mackenzie Country

Named after James Mackenzie, a Scotsman convicted of sheep rustling in 1855 with the help of a legendary Gaelic-only sheepdog named Friday — he escaped custody three times before being pardoned and vanishing; Friday, deemed too clever to be a normal dog, was hanged by suspicious locals. The name has since expanded to cover this whole vast, windswept high-country basin of tussock and braided rivers, one of the most distinctive landscapes in New Zealand.

Good to Know

Mackenzie Country: named after James Mackenzie, a Scottish settler accused in 1855 of stealing 1,000 sheep. He allegedly used his extraordinarily trained Gaelic-only sheepdog Friday. Convicted but escaped prison three times before pardon. The vast high-altitude tussock landscape named after him is one of New Zealand's most distinctive.

Canterbury

Twizel

Built in the 1960s as a temporary construction camp for the Waitaki Power Scheme, and saved from planned demolition by residents who fought to keep the town after the project finished. Now a comfortable base for the Mackenzie Country, with salmon farming in the nearby canal system.

Good to Know

Twizel: named by surveyor JT Thomson after a Northumberland bridge near the Battle of Flodden site (1513). Built as a temporary construction camp for the Waitaki Power Scheme (1960s); saved from planned demolition by local residents. Now a base for exploring the Mackenzie Country and near salmon farming operations in the Waitaki canal system.

Canterbury

Lake Pukaki

One of New Zealand's most celebrated views — Aoraki/Mt Cook and Mt Tasman visible at the southern end on a clear day. The lake's vivid, almost luminous blue comes from glacial flour, fine rock particles ground up by the Tasman Glacier and suspended in the water; the colour fades the further downstream you follow the Waitaki River as the flour settles out.

Good to Know

Lake Pukaki: Te reo Māori — pukaki (the source) — headwaters of the Waitaki River. The lake's iridescent blue colour is caused by glacial flour — microscopic rock particles from the Tasman Glacier suspended in the water. On clear days Aoraki/Mt Cook and Mt Tasman are visible at the lake's southern end. One of New Zealand's most celebrated views.

Canterbury

Mt Cook National Park

Home to 27 of New Zealand's 3,000m-plus peaks, including Aoraki/Mt Cook (3,763m, the country's highest) and Mt Tasman. Neither Cook nor Tasman ever actually saw these mountains — cloud blocked Cook's view both times he passed, and Tasman never sailed this far south. No Māori is recorded as having climbed Aoraki, since the summit is considered tapu. First scaled on Christmas Day 1894 by three New Zealanders racing to beat an Englishman to the top. Hillary trained here before his 1953 Everest climb. The Tasman Glacier, NZ's longest at 30km, is accessible from Mt Cook Village, though Franz Josef and Fox on the coast offer more developed glacier tours.

Good to Know

Aoraki/Mt Cook National Park: 27 of NZ's 28 peaks over 3,000 metres. Aoraki/Mt Cook (3,763m) is New Zealand's highest peak; Mt Tasman is second. Neither Cook nor Tasman ever saw the mountains named after them — Tasman never sailed this far south, cloud obscured Cook's view on both passes. UNESCO World Heritage Site. Tasman Glacier (NZ's longest) offers guided walks and ski-plane landings.

Canterbury

Lake Tekapo

A stunning lakeside town that books out fast in both summer and winter. Home to the Church of the Good Shepherd (1935, built from local stone, framed windows looking straight out over the lake) and a bronze sheepdog memorial. Sits within the Mackenzie Basin Dark Sky Reserve — one of the best stargazing spots in the world, with nightly tours up Mt John Observatory. Also home to an NZDF Antarctic survival training base, and Irishman's Creek Station, birthplace of Bill Hamilton's jet boat in 1954.

Good to Know

Lake Tekapo: misspelling of takapo — taka (floor mat), po (night) — recalling the night Rākaihautu's party fled in darkness. The Church of the Good Shepherd is one of NZ's most photographed buildings. The Mackenzie Basin Dark Sky Reserve offers outstanding stargazing; Mt John Observatory runs evening tours. Accommodation fills quickly — advance booking essential.

Canterbury

Fairlie

A junction town, named after a Scottish honeymoon spot. Turn left here for the faster route to Christchurch via Geraldine, or continue straight for the detour to Waitohi, site of an early aviation milestone — worth it if you've got the time and curiosity.

Good to Know

Fairlie: named 1884 by David Hamilton after Fairlie, Ayrshire, Scotland, his honeymoon location. A junction point: left toward Geraldine leads to Christchurch; continuing through leads to Waitohi, site of an important early aviation milestone. Good café and services for travellers.

Canterbury

Waitohi

Where reclusive farmer Richard Pearse — dismissed locally as 'Mad Pearse' — attempted a powered flight in his home-built plane on 31 March, possibly in 1903, months before the Wright Brothers. The date is disputed and the flight itself was uncontrolled, ending in a hedge, but Pearse's self-built engine and design features (ailerons, tricycle undercarriage, low-mounted monoplane wing) were genuinely ahead of their time. A roadside monument with a replica aircraft marks the spot.

Good to Know

Waitohi: Te reo Māori — a baptismal rite performed in a river. In 1903 or 1904 (date disputed) farmer Richard Pearse, locally called Mad Pearse, achieved one of the world's first powered controlled flights. Whether this preceded the Wright Brothers' December 1903 flight is contested. Largely unwitnessed. A roadside monument with a replica aircraft commemorates him.

Canterbury

Temuka

Birthplace of Phar Lap, arguably the greatest racehorse in history — won 37 of 51 starts including the 1930 Melbourne Cup, before dying suddenly and mysteriously in California in 1932. Modern tests confirm arsenic in his system, though whether from poisoning, taxidermy, or period animal husbandry practices remains unresolved. His hide, heart and skeleton are split between museums in Melbourne, Canberra and Wellington; a bronze statue stands at the nearby Phar Lap Raceway.

Good to Know

Temuka: corruption of Te Umukaha — te (the), umu (earth oven), kaha (strong). Birthplace of Phar Lap (born Seadown, 4 October 1926), widely regarded as one of the greatest racehorses ever. Won 37 of 51 starts including the 1930 Melbourne Cup. Death in California 1932 remains unexplained. His hide is at Museum Victoria (Melbourne), skeleton at Te Papa (Wellington), and heart at the National Museum (Canberra).

Canterbury

Geraldine

A well-known, well-serviced rest stop between Fairlie and Christchurch. Once home to the (since-beaten) world's largest knitted jersey, and to a 33-year, two-million-piece steel mosaic recreation of the Bayeux Tapestry, now on permanent display in Bayeux, France. Rangitata Rafts nearby offers whitewater rafting from complete beginner to grade five.

Good to Know

Geraldine: named after the Irish clan form of FitzGerald, honouring district councillor James Edward FitzGerald. Well-located rest stop between Fairlie and Christchurch. Formerly held the Guinness World Record for the World's Largest Knitted Jersey, created by local residents Michael and Gillian Linton.

Canterbury

Rakaia

Sits beside one of the finest braided river systems in the world — channels constantly shift and reconnect across a wide shingle bed. The SH1 bridge, completed in 1939, was the longest road bridge in the Southern Hemisphere at 1.8km when built. Well known for salmon fishing.

Good to Know

Rakaia: Te reo Māori — arranged in ranks, referring to men crossing the river in formation to break its force. The Rakaia River is one of the world's finest braided river examples. The SH 1 bridge (1.8km, built 1939) was the Southern Hemisphere's longest road bridge at the time. Renowned for salmon fishing.

Canterbury

Christchurch

Planned as an English city on a grid across the flat Canterbury Plains, much as Dunedin was planned Scottish. Home town of Kate Sheppard, whose campaign won New Zealand women the vote in 1893 — the first self-governing country to do so; she's on the $10 note. Struck by two major earthquakes — Darfield in September 2010 (no deaths) and a shallower, more destructive quake on 22 February 2011 that killed 185 people, New Zealand's deadliest disaster since 1931. The rebuild has produced genuinely interesting new architecture, though central-city accommodation can still be tight. The International Antarctic Centre near the airport, largely undamaged, is well worth a visit — including a recreated Antarctic storm and Hagglund rides.

Good to Know

Christchurch: named after Christ Church College, Oxford, by ex-pupil John Robert Godley. Planned as an English city on a grid across flat Canterbury Plains. On 22 February 2011 a magnitude 6.3 earthquake killed 185 people — New Zealand's deadliest natural disaster since 1931. The ongoing central city rebuild has produced innovative architecture. Key attractions: Botanic Gardens, Cathedral precinct, Cardboard Cathedral.

Canterbury

Amberley

First sign of the Waipara wine region, about 45 minutes north of Christchurch — the vineyards themselves sit a little further up the road. Known for Pinot Noir, Riesling and Gewürztraminer from a warm, sheltered valley. Worth a short cellar-door detour if wine's on the agenda.

Good to Know

Amberley: named after a town in Oxfordshire, England, birthplace of the landowner who subdivided the site in the late 1850s. On SH 1, approximately 45 minutes north of Christchurch. Access point for the Waipara wine region (vineyards a few minutes further north), known for Pinot Noir, Riesling, and Gewürztraminer.

Canterbury

Cheviot

A sensible rest stop roughly two hours north of Christchurch and under an hour from Kaikōura. The road tightens and winds considerably as it nears the Kaikōura coast, so worth fuelling up and stretching your legs here first.

Good to Know

Cheviot: named after the nearby Cheviot Hills farm, itself named after the Scottish border country. Practical rest stop approximately two hours north of Christchurch and under an hour from Kaikōura. The road ahead becomes narrower and more winding — good point to stop, refuel, and stretch.

Canterbury

Kaikoura

The coastline's real draw: the sea floor drops away almost as steeply as the mountains rise, drawing an unusually rich range of marine life close to shore year-round. Whale Watch Kaikōura (2.5-3 hours, mostly sperm whales, departs from the old railway station) and Dolphin Encounter both come with refund/rebooking guarantees if they don't find what you came for. Locally caught crayfish is genuinely good, but prices vary — worth shopping around. The 2016 earthquake reshaped parts of this coastline.

Good to Know

Kaikōura: shortened from Te Ahi-kai-koura-a-Tamakiterangi — kai (food), koura (crayfish). The sea floor drops sharply offshore bringing diverse marine life close to shore year-round, including resident sperm whales, dolphins, and fur seals. The Kaikōura Ranges rise directly behind the town. The 2016 earthquake uplifted the seabed and reshaped the coastline. Key activities: whale watching, dolphin swimming, crayfish, fur seal viewing.

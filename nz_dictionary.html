How to pronounce Māori

The Māori language or te reo (the language) is an easy language to quickly get a basic pronunciation, especially for most people who have English as a second (third or fourth…) language.

The Māori alphabet has a reduced alphabet of 15 characters:

  • 8 consonants: h, k, m, n, p, r, t, w
  • 5 vowels: a, e, i, o, u
  • 2 digraphs: wh and ng

Pronunciation again is simple; it’s only difficult if you try to speak it as English. Here are a few tricks.

The first and most basic is to stop, read the word and say it as it is written. Except for the wh and ng, everything is said as it is seen. Every letter is pronounced… no silent letters.

  • A is pronounced ah
  • E is pronounced eh
  • I is pronounced eee
  • O is pronounced or
  • U is pronounced ooo

The vowels are pronounced as long vowels, like in German, Spanish, French and Dutch to name a few. NO SHORT vowel sounds, English speakers!

If the vowel is to be pronounced longer it will be written as a double letter (aa, ee, ii, oo, uu) or with a macron above it: ā, ē, ī, ō, ū.

But there is more to think about: some combinations of letters have their own pronunciation.

  • Wh is said like an f
  • Ng is like the ng in singer
  • R roll your r like a good Scotsman

Break each word just after the vowels and you should be able to pronounce Māori correctly.

A good example is the name of the people, “Māori” (it actually means normal person, and if you look at it, they’re right: until 1800 AD there were only Māori here, so Europeans were the odd ones). But back to the point.

Māori is not said “marry” like is the misconception (that’s what you do when you find a loved one). Just follow the rules above: ma/o/ri = ma o r(roll it)ee, simple!

Remember that a lot of New Zealanders don’t pronounce a lot of the words correctly themselves, especially some place names. You might actually be getting it more right than the locals, so don’t be put off, keep doing it right. With te reo programmes in schools being introduced in the 1980s and 90s, it’s taking time for mainstream Kiwis to adapt from years of getting it wrong.

Some local areas have their own take or dialect on the pronunciation of the wh and ng. If you use them as explained above, the locals will understand and will not rubbish you for making the right effort.
